\section{Onboard Processing Unit}

Onboard data processing is performed using a 
\textit{Raspberry Pi 5} in combination with a 
\textit{Hailo AI accelerator}. This configuration enables 
real-time processing of multimodal sensor data directly on 
the UAV.

The Raspberry Pi 5 is equipped with a quad-core ARM 
Cortex-A76 CPU (up to 2.4\,GHz) and provides improved I/O 
performance compared to previous generations. It supports 
high-speed interfaces such as PCIe 2.0, which allows direct 
integration of external AI accelerators. Depending on the 
configuration, the system is available with up to 8\,GB of 
LPDDR4X RAM. Typical power consumption of the Raspberry Pi 5 
ranges between 5\,W and 12\,W, depending on workload and 
connected peripherals.

The Hailo accelerator (e.g., Hailo-8) is a dedicated deep 
learning inference processor designed for edge applications. 
It delivers up to 26 tera-operations per second (TOPS) while 
maintaining low power consumption (typically 2.5–3.5\,W under 
load). The accelerator is optimized for convolutional neural 
networks (CNNs) and supports common deep learning frameworks 
through a dedicated software development kit (SDK).

The combination of the Raspberry Pi 5 and the Hailo accelerator 
provides a compact and energy-efficient embedded computing 
platform capable of executing neural network-based vehicle 
detection algorithms in real time. This architecture enables 
onboard inference, reducing the need for high-bandwidth data 
transmission to a ground station and thereby increasing 
operational autonomy and system responsiveness.

\subsection{Remote Sensing}


Remote sensing in the context of UAV-based vehicle detection is 
the acquisition of information about the Earth's surface and objects on it
without making physical contact. The remotely collected data can
be of many different types, yet the primary sensing approach in
autonomous systems and object detection applications is 
based on the detection of electromagnetic radiation.
\cite[p.~1]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}

Figure~\ref{fig:electromagnetic_remote_sensing} illustrates 
the elements involved in electromagnetic remote sensing of earth 
resources in airborne systems.  The elements of the data 
acquisition process are energy sources (a), propagation of energy 
through the atmosphere (b), energy interactions with earth surface 
features (c), retransmission of energy through the atmosphere (d), 
and sensors (e), which results in the generation 
of sensor data in pictorial form. 
\cite[p.~2]{lillesand2015remoteSensing} 
Note that passive systems rely on external sources of energy, such 
as reflected sunlight as in photography, or heat emitted by objects 
themselves as in thermal imaging, while active systems such as 
radar and LiDAR transmit their own energy and record the return signal 
\cite[p.~9,45]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{images/electromagnetic_remote_sensing_modified.png}
    \caption{
        Schematic representation of the generalized processes and elements involved in
        electromagnetic remote sensing. Modified after 
        \cite[p.~16]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}
    }
    \label{fig:electromagnetic_remote_sensing}
\end{figure}

Electromagnetic energy encompasses a broad range of radiation, all 
propagating as sinusoidal waves at the constant speed of light 
$c = 3 \times 10^8$~m/s. Each form of radiation is characterized by 
its wavelength $\lambda$, the distance between successive wave peaks,
 and frequency $f$, the number of wave cycles per second, 
which are inversely related through
\begin{equation}
    c = \lambda f.
\end{equation}
In remote sensing, radiation is typically categorized by wavelength, 
with the micrometer \si{\micro\meter} being the most common unit. 
The resulting electromagnetic spectrum and its named divisions, as 
shown in Figure~\ref{fig:electromagnetic_spectrum}, are organized 
largely by convenience rather than any inherent differences in energy 
characteristics --- the divisions have grown from the different methods 
developed for sensing each type of radiation rather than from 
clear-cut physical boundaries \cite[pp.~4]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}.


The spectral sensitivity of the human eye spans only the narrow visible 
portion of the spectrum, from approximately \SI{0.4}{\micro\meter} to 
\SI{0.7}{\micro\meter}, divided into blue (0.4--\SI{0.5}{\micro\meter}), 
green (0.5--\SI{0.6}{\micro\meter}), 
and red (0.6--\SI{0.7}{\micro\meter}). Ultraviolet 
(UV) energy adjoins the blue end of the visible range. Beyond the red 
end lies the infrared (IR) region, subdivided into near-IR 
(0.7--\SI{1.3}{\micro\meter}), mid-IR or shortwave IR 
(1.3--\SI{3}{\micro\meter}), and thermal or longwave IR 
(3--\SI{14}{\micro\meter}), where only the longwave portion is directly 
related to heat sensation, giving it's name. 
At much longer wavelengths, from \SI{1}{\milli\meter} to \SI{1}{\meter}, 
lies the microwave region. Most remote sensing systems operate in one 
or several of these regions: optical cameras in the visible, LiDAR 
typically at \SI{1.064}{\micro\meter} in the near-IR 
to avoid interference with 
the atmosphere and other sensors, thermal sensors in the thermal IR, 
and radar in the microwave region.
\cite[p.~6]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/electromagnetic_spectrum.png}
    \caption{
        The electromagnetic spectrum. The visible spectrum is 
        highlighted, and other important regions are indicated.
        \cite[p.~5]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}
    }
    \label{fig:electromagnetic_spectrum}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:electromagnetic_atmospheric_windows} illustrates the 
interrelationship between energy sources and atmospheric absorption 
characteristics. Figure~\ref{fig:electromagnetic_atmospheric_windows}a 
shows the spectral distribution of energy emitted by the sun and by earth 
features, the two most common natural energy sources in remote sensing. The 
energy of a wave is inversely proportional to its wavelength, meaning 
longer wavelengths carry lower energy content. This has important 
implications for remote sensing. Naturally emitted long-wavelength 
radiation, such as thermal infrared energy, 
is more difficult to sense than shorter-wavelength radiation, such as 
visible light. Consequently, systems 
operating at long wavelengths must view larger areas of the earth at any 
given time to obtain a detectable signal. Conversely, shorter wavelengths 
are easier to detect and provide more detailed information about the 
environment, such as material texture, but are also more susceptible to 
atmospheric absorption and scattering in suboptimal atmospheric conditions.
\cite[pp.~6, 9, 11]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}

The atmosphere does not transmit all wavelengths equally --- certain 
ranges, known as atmospheric windows, exhibit high transmittance and 
form the basis for remote sensing systems. In 
Figure~\ref{fig:electromagnetic_atmospheric_windows}b, spectral 
regions where the atmosphere blocks energy are shaded, and remote sensing 
data acquisition is restricted to the unshaded windows. Each sensing 
modality exploits a different window. Optical cameras operate in the 
visible range (\SIrange{0.4}{0.7}{\micro\meter}), which coincides with 
both an atmospheric window and the peak of solar energy output 
(Figure~\ref{fig:electromagnetic_atmospheric_windows}c), resulting in 
high-detail imagery under good atmospheric conditions. Thermal sensors 
detect emitted heat energy from the earth, shown by the small curve 
in (a), through windows at \SIrange{3}{5}{\micro\meter} and 
\SIrange{8}{14}{\micro\meter}. Its much lower intensity than solar 
radiation limits spatial detail, but the independence from solar 
illumination is a key advantage for detection in low-light conditions. 
LiDAR operates at 
\SI{1.064}{\micro\meter} in the near-infrared to minimise atmospheric 
interference, overlap with other sensors, and optimise detector material 
efficiency, though its proximity to the visible spectrum makes it more 
susceptible to scattering by fog and aerosols than radar.
% TODO: needs source (https://www.yellowscan.com/knowledge/why-does-lidar-have-a-specific-wavelength/)
Radar and passive microwave systems operate through a window spanning 
\SI{1}{\milli\meter} to \SI{1}{\meter}, where they can penetrate clouds 
and function entirely independently of solar illumination.
\cite[pp.~6, 10, 386, 481]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}

        (b) atmospheric transmittance, and
        (c) common remote sensing systems on

        Modified after 
        \cite[p.~11]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}

An ideal sensor would be highly sensitive to all wavelengths, yielding
spatially detailed radiance data across the entire electromagnetic
spectrum and wide areas on the ground. It would be simple and reliable,
require virtually no power or space, and be accurate and economical to
operate. Unsurprisingly, no such sensor exists: no single sensor is
sensitive to all wavelengths or energy levels, and all real sensors have
fixed limits of spatial, spectral, radiometric, and temporal resolution.
\cite[p.~46]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}
Therefore, in the following the available electromagnetic remote sensing modalities are
evaluated against the specific requirements of UAV-based vehicle
detection to identify the most suitable option, comparing how each
captures different aspects of the environment and how their performance
varies under different environmental conditions.

\paragraph{Visible-band Imaging}

Optical remote sensing is the oldest electromagnetic remote sensing method, 
originating with the invention of photography. We will refer to optical
remote sensors as cameras. Cameras are passive sensors that capture light
emitted by one or more sources, possibly reflected from object surfaces
multiple times, and directed towards the camera through the lens onto the
image sensor to form an image of the scene
as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:photometric_image_formation}b.
\cite[p.~66]{szeliski2022computerVision}
\cite{agrawal2019comparativeRemoteSensing}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/photometric_image_formation_szeliski_2021_lillesand_2015.png}
    \caption{
        (a) Spectral characteristics of  Simple lens camera, 
        \cite[p.~89]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}
        (b) A simplified model of photometric image formation.
        \cite[p.~66]{szeliski2022computerVision}
    }
    \label{fig:photometric_image_formation}
\end{figure}

The simple lens camera, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:photometric_image_formation}a, 
was developed to increase the amount of light reaching the image sensor within 
a given time, referred to as exposure. It achieves this by using a lens to 
gather light originating from a scene object over a larger area and converge it 
onto a single point on the sensor, provided the camera is properly focused on 
the object. The design and function of modern adjustable 
cameras is conceptually identical to the simple lens camera.
\cite[pp.~89]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}

To obtain sharp, properly exposed images, the camera must be focused and the 
correct exposure settings must be applied. In modern cameras, focus is achieved 
automatically by adjusting the position of the lens element. Beyond the lens 
itself, modern cameras introduce two additional controls: an adjustable diaphragm 
and an adjustable shutter. The diaphragm controls the diameter of the lens 
opening during exposure, while the shutter controls the duration of exposure.
\cite[pp.~89]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}

For a given camera and scene, exposure can be varied by changing the shutter 
speed $t$ and/or the lens opening diameter $d$, with various combinations of 
the two yielding equivalent exposures. Short exposure times allow one to freeze 
motion and prevent blurring when photographing moving objects, or when the camera 
itself is moving, as in the case of a UAV-mounted system. However, a shorter 
exposure time requires more light to reach the sensor per unit time, necessitating 
more illumination or a larger lens opening diameter. The largest available lens opening diameter is 
referred to as the lens speed, and determines the minimum exposure time achievable 
under a given lighting condition. A large lens opening diameter allows more light 
to reach the image sensor, making it beneficial under low-light conditions. A small 
lens opening diameter, on the other hand, limits overexposure and yields a larger range of distances over 
which objects appear sharp in the image, known as the depth of field. This is 
because the narrower diaphragm limits the angle of incoming light rays, forcing 
them to converge more directly onto the sensor.
\cite[pp.~89]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}
\cite[p.~81]{szeliski2022computerVision}

After light passes through the camera's lens, it reaches the imaging sensor, a 
two-dimensional array of photosensitive semiconductors. When light energy strikes each 
individual detector in this array, it produces a small electrical charge proportional 
to the brightness of the scene at that point. This charge is then converted into a 
voltage, and ultimately into a digital brightness value. However, these sensors are 
inherently monochromatic, so to capture color, each detector is covered by a colored 
filter most commonly arranged in a Bayer pattern (Figure~\ref{fig:bayer_filter}a). 
In this color filter array, half of the filters 
are green, with the remaining half split equally between red and blue. The overrepresentation 
of green takes advantage of both the green peak of solar radiation 
(Figure~\ref{fig:electromagnetic_atmospheric_windows}) and the human visual 
system's greater sensitivity to detail in brightness, which is largely determined by green, 
than in color. Since each detector only records 
one color, the two missing color values at every pixel must be interpolated from neighboring 
detectors of the same color — a process called demosaicing — to produce the final RGB 
image we see (Figure~\ref{fig:bayer_filter}b).
\cite[pp.~93]{szeliski2022computerVision}
\cite[pp.~115]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{images/bayer_filter_szeliski_2021_computer_vision.png}
    \caption{
        Bayer RGB pattern: (a) color filter array layout; (b) Pixel values,
        with interpolated values shown as lower case
        \cite[p.~94]{szeliski2022computerVision}
    }
    \label{fig:bayer_filter}
\end{figure}

Cameras are the most widely used remote sensing modality in autonomous 
traffic applications for good reason. RGB cameras offer exceptionally 
high spatial resolution and capture rich semantic information, including 
color, texture, shape, and fine-grained patterns over distances of several 
hundred meters \cite{wu2025comparativeAnalysisLiDARRadarCamera}. This 
detailed visual content enables tasks such as reading road signs, identifying 
lane markings, and recognizing traffic states, many of which are fundamentally 
inaccessible to purely geometric sensors or sensors operating outside the 
visible spectrum.
Their low cost, small form factor, and low energy consumption
have made them a widely adopted sensing modality in autonomous
traffic applications.
Their ubiquity has been further amplified by rapid advances in deep learning 
and convolutional neural networks, which have significantly improved scene 
object detection performance.
\cite{soltanirad2025autonomousTransportationPerception}
\cite{bustos2023reviewObjectDetectionIR}

Despite these strengths, reliance on visual-spectrum imaging alone is 
insufficient for robust perception. Cameras, although equipped with 
exposure and gain controls to adapt to varying illumination, remain 
passive sensors and are therefore fundamentally dependent on ambient 
light. In low-light conditions, such as nighttime, their ability to 
capture clear and detailed imagery degrades substantially, leading to 
reduced contrast and diminished detection performance. While vehicle    
headlights partially compensate by illuminating the roadway ahead, they 
provide limited illumination of the vehicle itself, restricting their 
usefulness for vision-based vehicle detection. Moreover, headlights are 
not a reliable discriminative feature. Many objects emit or reflect light, 
headlights may be occluded from certain viewpoints, particularly in 
side-view observations, or may not be active at all.

In addition, adverse weather conditions, including fog, rain, and snow, 
further degrade image quality by scattering and absorbing light during 
atmospheric transmission. High dynamic range scenes, such as environments 
with direct sunlight or deep shadows, also present challenges, 
as cameras often struggle to simultaneously preserve details in both 
brightly illuminated and dark regions. Glare and strong backlighting may 
lead to overexposure and localized loss of detail. Moreover, mirror 
reflections and lens contamination caused by dirt or moisture 
hinder reliable vehicle detection. Examples of these conditions are 
illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:camera_lighting_defects}.
To address these limitations, alternative remote sensing modalities 
aimed at enabling more robust vehicle detection beyond the constraints 
of the visible spectrum are explored in the following.
\cite{kenk2020dawn}
\cite{yahiaoui2020sunGlareDetection}
\cite{youngwan2026rasmd}
\cite{jia2021llvip}

\paragraph{Infrared Imaging}

Infrared imaging is a passive remote sensing modality that captures electromagnetic 
radiation in the infrared spectrum, which lies just beyond the visible range. Unlike 
visible-band imaging, which relies on reflected sunlight or artificial illumination, 
infrared sensors detect radiation emitted or reflected by objects themselves. This 
independence from external light sources enables detection both during daylight and 
at night, without reliance on solar illumination. While the optical design principles
of infrared sensors closely resembles that of optical cameras, the detector array is 
engineered to respond to the longer wavelengths characteristic of infrared radiation. 
The infrared spectrum spans several distinct subregions, each with different physical 
properties and sensing applications, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:IR_spectrum}.
\cite[pp.~243]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{images/IR_radiation_classification_modified.png}
    \caption{
        A visualization of the electromagnetic spectrum showcasing 
        different sections attributed to IR wavelengths. Modified after
        \cite{rizk2025humandDetectionThermalIR}
    }
    \label{fig:IR_spectrum}
\end{figure}

Of particular relevance to infrared imaging is the concept of blackbody radiation.
All objects emit thermal radiation as a function of their temperature, and the 
blackbody model describes this behavior for an ideal radiator that fully absorbs 
and reemits all incident energy.
As Figure~\ref{fig:black_body_radiation} shows, both the total emitted energy and its
spectral distribution vary with temperature: higher temperatures produce greater
total radiation and shift the peak toward shorter wavelengths, a relationship known
as Wien's displacement law. The sun approximates a 6000~K blackbody, with a spectral
peak near 0.5~$\mu$m, while Earth's surface at ${\sim}300$~K or 27~$^\circ$C peaks at 
${\sim}9.7~\mu$m. Consequently, the long-wave infrared
(LWIR) band (8--14~$\mu$m) is of particular interest for object detection, as it
captures thermal radiation emitted by objects at typical Earth surface temperatures,
enabling the detection of targets that exhibit a thermal contrast with, or elevated
temperature relative to, their surroundings such as vehicles and personnel.
\cite[pp.~7,~218]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}

Thermal imaging in the long-wave infrared spectrum overcomes
many of the limitations of visible-band imaging previously illustrated
in Figure~\ref{fig:camera_lighting_defects}. Unlike RGB sensors, LWIR
cameras are unaffected by changes in visible illumination, enabling
reliable detection of vehicles and pedestrians in low-light and
nighttime conditions. In full darkness, LWIR is the only passive
sensor capable of pedestrian detection. In foggy conditions, LWIR
similarly maintains reliable detection where visible-band cameras
fail, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:comparison_recognition_vis_lwir}.
Since LWIR cameras are inherently insensitive to visible light, they
are equally immune to glare from direct sunlight, vehicle headlights,
reflections, and high-contrast backlighting, all of which can cause
overexposure and loss of detail in visible-band
imagery.
\cite{pinchon2018allWeatherVision}

Compared to NIR and SWIR, LWIR also performs better in adverse
weather. In fog combined with oncoming headlights, VIS, NIR, and SWIR
sensors are susceptible to glare severe enough to render vehicle
recognition impossible. LWIR delivers more consistent recognition
results regardless of whether a vehicle's headlights are on or off.
Due to its longer wavelength, LWIR is less affected by atmospheric
scattering from fog, snow, and sand than NIR, SWIR, and visible-band
cameras~\cite{pinchon2018allWeatherVision}. However, LWIR is not
entirely immune to atmospheric degradation. Water vapor in fog and
rain absorbs and scatters infrared radiation, reducing thermal
contrast and detection range in very dense
fog~\cite{rivera2022analysisThermalExtremeFog}. LWIR should therefore
be regarded as more robust than visible, NIR, and SWIR imaging under
adverse conditions, rather than completely unaffected by them. These
properties are illustrated in

Thermal imaging, however, comes with its own set of limitations. Compared to RGB sensors,
thermal cameras generally offer lower spatial resolution, since the low energy content and
low emitted radiation of long-wavelength radiation, as seen in Fig.~\ref{fig:black_body_radiation},
requires sensing larger areas to obtain a detectable signal. This also results in generally
lower image contrast than in the visible spectrum \cite[p.~6-8]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}.
Thermal images also lack the visual detail, texture, and color information that visible
cameras provide, all of which are important cues for object recognition and classification
\cite{bustos2023reviewObjectDetectionIR}.

Environmental conditions further constrain thermal performance by reducing the temperature
difference between objects and their surroundings.Wind accelerates the cooling of warm objects and rain causes water to absorb much of the 
objects thermal energy, both producing a more uniform background and diminishing contrast
\cite{stlaurent2007combinationColorThermal, bustos2023reviewObjectDetectionIR}. 
Solar heating further affects thermal contrast, as it warms objects differently depending 
on their thermal inertia. Asphalt and vehicles
heat up considerably during the day, while water, trees, and people change comparatively
little \cite{stlaurent2007combinationColorThermal, choi2018kaist}. In summer, this can
reduce the temperature contrast between vehicles and background as the entire scene heats up, 
lowering thermal
performance precisely when optical cameras perform very well under clear-sky conditions.
An illustrative example is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:thermal_contrast_sunny_rainy}, where the reduction
in thermal contrast during rain and sunny days is clearly visible \cite{bustos2023reviewObjectDetectionIR}.

\paragraph{LiDAR}

LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) is an active remote sensing technique 
for range-finding. It projects an optical signal onto a target and detects the
reflected or backscattered return. By processing this return signal, the
system determines the distance to the target and constructs a 3D point cloud
of the surrounding environment, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:lidar_point_cloud}.
LiDAR systems typically employ laser sources in the near-infrared spectral region,
with wavelengths between 0.80 and 1.55\,\textmu m. This range coincides with
an atmospheric transmission window, where water absorption is low. These
wavelengths are also invisible to the human eye, which is an important
safety consideration \cite{royo2019overviewLiDARAutonomousVehicles}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/lidar_point_cloud_melotti_2020_multimodal_recognition.png}
    \caption{
        Example obtained from KITTI 2D Object
        Detection Dataset. The 3D point cloud is projected onto 
        the image and coloured proportionally to the measured distance.
        \cite{melotti2020multimodalCameraLiDARRecognition}
    }
    \label{fig:lidar_point_cloud}
\end{figure}

Three main measurement approaches exist: pulsed time-of-flight (TOF),
amplitude-modulated continuous wave (AMCW), and frequency-modulated
continuous wave (FMCW). Pulsed TOF is currently the most widely used
technology in automotive LiDAR systems and is therefore the focus of this
section.
\cite{royo2019overviewLiDARAutonomousVehicles}
In pulsed TOF LiDAR, a short laser pulse is emitted toward a target. The
system records the elapsed time until the reflected echo returns to the
detector. Since the speed of light $c$ is a known constant, the range $R$ to
the target is computed as:

\begin{equation}
    R = \frac{1}{2} \cdot c \cdot t_{\mathrm{TOF}}
    \label{eq:lidar_range}
\end{equation}

where $t_{\mathrm{TOF}}$ is the round-trip travel time of the pulse. The
factor of two accounts for the pulse travelling the distance to the target
twice: once on the outward path and once on the return path. This principle
is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:lidar_tof}.
The ambiguity distance arises when a second pulse is
emitted before the echo of the first has returned. In this case, the system
cannot reliably assign the echo to the correct pulse. To avoid this, each
new pulse must be transmitted only after the return of the previous one has
been received. The ambiguity
distance is therefore inversely related to the pulse repetition rate, which
can be seen in Equation~\ref{eq:lidar_range}.
\cite[pp.~25]{wang2024introductionLiDARRemoteSensing}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/lidar_tof_wang_2024_introduction_lidar.png}
    \caption{
        Pulsed time-of-flight measurement principle. A laser pulse is
        emitted toward the target and the round-trip travel time
        $t$ of the reflected echo is measured to determine
        the range $R$.
        \cite{royo2019overviewLiDARAutonomousVehicles}
    }
    \label{fig:lidar_tof}
\end{figure}


Two factors constrain the maximum measurable object recognition range in LiDAR systems.
The first is atmospheric scattering, which causes the laser pulse to lose
energy as it travels, leaving only a small fraction of emitted light to
return to the detector.
\cite{royo2019overviewLiDARAutonomousVehicles}
This effect is worsened by adverse weather conditions such as fog, snow,
and rain, analogous to the scattering behavior previously described for
near-infrared cameras, as LiDAR operates in the same electromagnetic range.
In such conditions, suspended particles redirect a portion of the emitted
pulse back toward the sensor before it reaches the target, a phenomenon
known as backscatter.
At a visibility of \SI{25}{\meter}, backscatter reduces the perceivable
range to approximately \SI{6}{\meter} and introduces numerous false
measurements, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:fog_chamber_rgb_lidar_radar}.
Therefore, LiDAR is a viable solution for night driving, yet unreliable
under adverse weather conditions.
\cite{bijelic2020seeingThroughFog}
\cite{kim2023analysisLiDARPerformanceFogRain}


\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/fog_chamber_rgb_lidar_radar_bijelic_2020_seeing_through_fog.png}
    \caption{
        Multimodal sensor response of RGB camera, scanning lidar, and radar 
        in a fog chamber with dense fog. Reference recordings under clear 
        conditions shown in the first row, recordings in fog with 
        visibility of \SI{23}{\meter} shown in the second row.
        \cite{bijelic2020seeingThroughFog}
    }
    \label{fig:fog_chamber_rgb_lidar_radar}
\end{figure}

The second factor limiting the maximum object recognition range is the sparsity
and limited information content of point clouds. Unlike RGB images, point clouds
lack rich semantic cues such as texture and color information, which limits
object recognition capabilities compared to camera-based methods. 
Beyond this, the structure of point cloud data itself
poses difficulties. Unlike the regular 2D pixel grid of a camera image, 3D
points are unordered and lack fixed neighborhoods, making local feature
extraction sensitive to small perturbations, especially at larger distances. 
This is further compounded by sparsity, which is severely imbalanced 
across objects and worsens with distance, as illustrated in 
Figure~\ref{fig:sparse_point_cloud}. At greater distances, 
fewer points are captured and spatial shapes become increasingly 
incomplete, causing small or thin objects like pedestrians to be 
potentially missed entirely.
\cite{wang2023lidarOnly3DObjectDetection}
\cite{qi2020imvotenet}

    \caption{

        \cite{wang2023lidarOnly3DObjectDetection}
    }
    \label{fig:sparse_point_cloud}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Radar}

The basic operating principle of imaging radar (Radio Detection and Ranging) is 
largely similar to that of LiDAR systems. While LiDAR emits laser pulses in the 
near-infrared spectrum and detects the reflected light using photodetectors, radar 
systems transmit radio waves in the microwave range and detect the reflected signals 
using antennas.
Imaging radar emits short microwave pulses in a narrow beam from its antennas. These 
waves propagate through space, reflect off objects, and return as echoes, as illustrated 
in Figure~\ref{fig:radar_principle}. The received signals are then processed by the 
radar receiver, where the signal processing chain is largely based on the Fourier 
transform, which converts the raw wave signals measured by antenna arrays 
into spatial and motion-related information. From this processing, the range of 
objects is estimated from the time delay of the returned signal, while the signal 
strength provides information about the reflectivity of targets, which depends on 
properties such as size, shape, and material. In addition, the relative radial velocity of 
objects can be estimated using the Doppler shift of the returned signal. 
To localize objects in the environment, angular 
information is obtained from the antenna array geometry using phase differences 
across antennas, which allows estimation of the 
vertical angle (elevation) along the vertical antenna dimension and the horizontal 
angle (azimuth) along the horizontal antenna dimension.
\cite[pp.~389, 400]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}
\cite{kurniawan2023clusterfusionRadarCamera3DObjectDetection}
\cite{srivastav2023radarsAutonomousDriving}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/radar_principle_lillesand_2015_remote_sensing.png}
    \caption{
        Operating principle of imaging radar: (a) propagation of one radar
        pulse wavefront location at multiple time intervals; (b) resulting
        antenna return.
        Modified after \cite[p.~390]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}.
    }
    \label{fig:radar_principle}
\end{figure}

Radar data can be represented in several formats extracted at different
stages of the Fourier-transform-based signal processing chain, including
the range-azimuth-Doppler (RAD) tensor, range-azimuth heatmap, point cloud,
and micro-Doppler spectrogram (rarely used for object detection because it
does not provide spatial information), as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:radar_formats}. 
Modern 4D radars capture observations across range, Doppler, azimuth, and elevation,
producing a four-dimensional radar tensor. Earlier 3D radar systems commonly
store observations in a three-dimensional range-azimuth-Doppler tensor,
where measurements are averaged over the elevation dimension if present.
This representation allows object position, speed, and coarse 2D shape to
be inferred directly from the tensor, because the azimuth and elevation
resolution are typically low.
Due to their large size, RAD tensors are impractical for deep learning and
are often compressed. A common example is
the range-azimuth heatmap, which forms a 2D radar image (top-down view) by
collapsing the Doppler dimension. Each bin contains the amplitude of the
observations as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:range_azimuth_heatmap}, 
occasionally including collapsed Doppler information. This
representation still enables localization of objects in the top-down view,
but loses the ability to represent multiple velocities at the same spatial
location, retaining only an average Doppler estimate per location.
\cite{srivastav2023radarsAutonomousDriving}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/radar_formats_2023_srivastav_radars_autonomous_vehicles.png}
    \caption{

        (a) Range-Azimuth-Doppler tensor, 
        (b) Range-Azimuth heatmap,
        (c) Radar point cloud showing strong observations from the radar cube,
        (d) Micro-Doppler spectrogram capturing temporal variations of object motion.
        \cite{srivastav2023radarsAutonomousDriving}
    }
    \label{fig:radar_formats}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/range_azimuth_map_2021_wang_rodnet.png}
    \caption{
        Example Images of range-azimuth heatmaps with corresponding RGB images.
        Leftmost image shows two pedestrians detected as a single azimuth peak.
        Right image shows detection of occluded vehicle.
        Rightmost images shows detection independent of sunlight.
        \cite{wang2021rodnetRadarObjectDetection}
    }
    \label{fig:range_azimuth_heatmap}
\end{figure}

While the formats mentioned above are commonly used to represent radar data, 
object detection commonly relies on radar point clouds for input data. The 
point cloud represents relevant observations within the radar cube as a sparse 
set of data points in 3D space, since objects typically occupy only small 
subspaces of the radar tensor. Each point corresponds to a peak in the radar 
tensor obtained using a constant false alarm rate (CFAR) peak extraction 
algorithm. CFAR classifies a cell in the radar cube as containing a signal, that 
is an echo, if its amplitude relative to the average noise floor computed from 
surrounding cells exceeds a predefined threshold. Each point in the cloud 
contains both spatial features describing its 3D location consisting of range, 
azimuth, and elevation if present, and radar specific attributes such as radial 
velocity.
\cite{srivastav2023radarsAutonomousDriving}

Radar point clouds are represented either in a bird's eye view (BEV) or in a 
perspective view, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:radar_point_cloud_formats}. 
In the BEV representation, points are accumulated in a top down grid that includes 
observations even when they are occluded. In contrast, the perspective view 
represents the radar data from the viewpoint of the radar sensor and is similar 
to camera images from the ego vehicle, but with an additional depth feature and 
an associated velocity assigned to each pixel. However, radar 
perspective images have low and irregular spatial resolution due to the limited 
azimuth and elevation resolution of radar sensors. Moreover, the capability to 
capture occluded objects is lost in the perspective view.
\cite{srivastav2023radarsAutonomousDriving}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/radar_point_cloud_formats_2023_srivastav_radars_autonomous_vehicles.png}
    \caption{
        Radar point cloud data input formats used for deep learning
        models. The bird-eye view (BEV) (a) in a top-down grid,
        while the perspective view (b) projects data as seen from an image.
        \cite{srivastav2023radarsAutonomousDriving}
    }
    \label{fig:radar_point_cloud_formats}
\end{figure}

Overall, radar sensors provide high resolution information about object distance
and velocity, as well as low-resolution estimates of shape, size, and
material properties, which is beneficial for object detection.
Since radar systems operate using radio waves of roughly \SI{4}{\milli\meter} 
rather than visible or
infrared light, they are largely insensitive to lighting conditions
and remain robust in low-visibility environments such as fog, dust,
or moderate rain. Their relatively large wavelength results in minimal
scattering by small particles, allowing radar signals to propagate
through adverse weather conditions with little attenuation, as
illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:signal_attenuation_radar_lidar}.
This also enables radar to detect objects over long distances and
to observe objects under partial occlusion.
\cite{kurniawan2023clusterfusionRadarCamera3DObjectDetection}
\cite{karangwa2023reviewVehicleDetectionAutonomousDriving}
\cite{srivastav2023radarsAutonomousDriving}

\begin{figure}[H]

    }

\end{figure}

However, radar sensors typically provide low angular resolution due to 
antenna array size constraints and therefore produce sparse and partial 
point clouds of detected objects. This sparsity makes reliable object 
recognition very difficult when radar data is used alone and often 
requires fusion with complementary sensors such as cameras or LiDAR, 
which limits the overall weather robustness to that of the fused sensors. 
In addition, the large radar wavelength can cause nearby surfaces, such as 
the ground or guardrails, to act as specular reflectors (i.e. mirrors), 
producing spurious observations in radar data known as clutter, as seen in 
Figure~\ref{fig:radar_multipath_clutter}, which may result in false positives.
\cite{kurniawan2023clusterfusionRadarCamera3DObjectDetection}
\cite{karangwa2023reviewVehicleDetectionAutonomousDriving}
\cite{srivastav2023radarsAutonomousDriving}

Based on this evaluation, a multimodal configuration combining
RGB and LWIR imaging was selected. Optical imaging provide the
the most informative visual features under favorable lighting conditions
of all modalities. 
The inclusion of a thermal camera provides a way to 
fill the blind spots in optical environmental perception
in low-light and adverse weather conditions.
To make up for the limitations of optical cameras, LWIR imaging was selected 
as a complementary modality because of its independence of external 
illumination and robustness to atmospheric scattering in adverse weather
due to it's longer wavelength.
For these reasons, we can expect to obtain
better performance by using the strong points of the color
and the thermal images throughout the day.
LiDAR was disregarded due to the fact that its performance
degrades significantly in fog, rain, and snow, which are precisely the
conditions under which RGB cameras also fail as seen in Table~\ref{tab:modality_comparison}. The
NIR spectrum 
used by LiDAR lies very close to the visible spectrum.
Radar was also disregarded due to its low spatial resolution, 
which requires fusion with complementary 
sensors such as cameras or LiDAR to achieve reliable object 
recognition, which would limit the overall weather robustness 
to that of the fused sensors.
The selected RGB and LWIR modalities provide a good balance of 
complementary strengths and weaknesses for vehicle detection,
though they are not without limitations as previously discussed.
It was deemed that the combination of these two modalities would provide a
sufficiently rich data source for
object detection under a wide range of conditions.

A big limitation of this configuration is that it does not include 
active sensors such as LiDAR or radar, which provide depth information.
This would be very beneficial for the later stage of traffic jam
classification, as it would allow for more accurate estimation of the
distance to detected vehicles and their relative speed, which are important 
cues for traffic jam classification. For this reason, a laser-based 
distance measurement was desired in the selected hardware platform, 
which can provide some depth information. An initial idea was to
integrate this modality with the existing RGB and LWIR sensors to
give the model depth information which can be used to better predict
vehicle bounding box sizes. However, it was decided that this modality is not 
used for object detection in this project 
due to the fact that it only provides a single distance measurement
in the center of the field of view which can be vastly different 
from the actual position of detected vehicles, making it unsuitable 
for estimating vehicle size in the image, and is not robust to adverse weather conditions, as it 
operates in the same near-infrared spectrum as LiDAR. Therefore, 
it was decided that the laser-based distance measurement would be 
reserved for later use in traffic jam classification.

Based on this specification, sensor data acquisition is performed using the 
\textit{Workswell WIRIS Enterprise} seen in Figure~\ref{fig:WIRIS_Enterprise}. 
The WIRIS Enterprise integrates the three sensing 
modalities: RGB imaging at resolution 1920\,×\,1080 pixels, thermal imaging
 at resolution 640\,×\,512 pixels, and laser-based 
central distance measurement along the optical axis of up to 1500\,m.
These values are below the maximum specifications listed for the
WORKswell WIRIS Enterprise, but they are the maximum that can be achieved
during simultaneous video recording and are sufficient for the intended application, as the RGB 
and LWIR resolutions are comparable to those of existing datasets used 
for training and evaluation of vehicle detection models.
This multi-sensor system is
specifically designed for use with UAVs and significantly reduces
the complexity of mounting the three sensors on the drone by requiring
only a single gimbal.
Its weight is specified at less than 
680\,g, and its dimensions are approximately 76\,×\,107\,×\,102\,mm. 
The system operates with a supply voltage between 9\,V and 36\,V 
DC and has a typical power consumption of approximately 
18\,W (peak consumption around 21\,W). These specifications 
allow integration into the drone's power system with 
appropriate battery capacity planning.
\cite{worksellWIRISspec2022}

A primary reason for the \textit{Workswell WIRIS Enterprise} is the
out-of-the-box spatial alignment it provides between these two sensor types.
This is an advantage as RGB and infrared sensors generally suffer from
inherent misalignment because they occupy different physical positions on a
device, resulting in different coordinate systems, mismatched fields of view,
and differing sampling frequencies that introduce temporal offsets — and for
moving objects, these temporal gaps result in spatial misalignment as well.
Many existing multi-modal fusion methods assume that visible-infrared image
pairs are perfectly geometrically aligned and fuse features directly, but in
practice, paired images remain only weakly aligned, with objects differing 
not only in position but also in scale and angle, as illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:rgb_thermal_misalignment}.
The practical severity of this problem is well documented: in the DroneVehicle
dataset, which provides RGB-thermal image pairs captured from a UAV
perspective, more than 20\% of bounding boxes exhibit misalignment defined as
a position or size offset of at least 3 pixels or an angular offset of at
least 3 degrees.
Such misalignment directly degrades detection performance, producing
low-quality bounding box predictions and undermine fusion methods that rely
on aligned feature maps.
\cite{yuan2022crossModalAlignmentVehicleDetection}
\cite{he2023misalignedRGBTadaptiveCalibration}

more information for detection.

\section{Sensor Alignment}
\label{chap:sensor_alignment}

The sensor data exhibited two main issues that needed to be addressed:
temporal misalignment and spatial misalignment between the RGB and thermal
video streams.

\paragraph{Temporal Misalignment}
The temporal misalignment was primarily caused by differences in the sampling 
rates of the two cameras. The RGB camera captured frames at 20\,fps, whereas 
the thermal
camera operated at 21.2\,fps. As a result, a direct frame-by-frame overlay
was not possible, since the streams gradually drift apart over time. For
instance, the ratio $20 / 21.2 \approx 0.9434$ leads to a temporal drift of
approximately $3.4$\,seconds after one minute of recording.
Additionally, the recordings from both cameras did not start simultaneously,
introducing a constant temporal offset between the two streams.

To address these issues, a two-step synchronization process was applied.
First, the thermal video was temporally aligned to the RGB video by matching
frames based on their timestamps. For each RGB frame, the closest thermal
frame in time was identified using the presentation timestamps stored in the
frame metadata. These timestamps were converted from discrete clock ticks to
seconds by applying the corresponding timebase of each video stream. The
thermal frame with the smallest temporal difference to the RGB frame was then
selected, resulting in two videos with equal numbers of synchronized frames.

While this step eliminated the progressive temporal drift, a constant offset
between the videos remained due to different recording start times. This
offset was estimated by identifying a distinct and easily recognizable event
in both videos (e.g., two cars crossing, as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:event}). The difference between the corresponding timestamps
yielded an offset of 2.6\,seconds.
The final synchronization was achieved by shifting one video by this offset
and repeating the frame-matching procedure. This resulted in temporally
aligned video streams with a maximum frame pairing error of 55\,ms over
15-minute recordings.

The first problem that needed to be addressed for the sensor data
was temporal misalignment in the collected video samples during data
collection. This was due to the fact that the optical camera sampled
frames at a rate of 20 fps, while the thermal camera provides 21.2 fps.
This means that overlaying frame by frame was not possible as this
leads to significant temporal mismatch. For example, after 1 minute with
20/21.2 = 0.9434 which leads to a difference of 60s * 0.9434 = 3.4 seconds
after 1 minute already.
In addition, it was found that the videos collected during data collection did not start
at the same time, which means that the temporal misalignment is also because of this.

The first step to address this problem was to synchronize the videos by first
converting the thermal video to the same frame rate as the RGB video. This was done by
iterating through the RGB video frame by frame and for each RGB frame, 
finding the closest thermal frame in time by using the presentation timestamp
included in the metadata of the video frames, multiplying this timestamp 
by the corresponding timebase of the two videos to convert it from ticks, which are
integer time units used by media stream clocks, to seconds, and then finding the 
thermal frame with the closest second timestamp to the RGB frame timestamp and matching
them together, creating two videos with the same number of synchronized frames.

After this process, the videos were no longer drifting apart temporally over time, yet
there was still a constant temporal offset between the two videos due to the differing
start times. This offset was estimated by selecting a well suited and easily identifiable
event in both videos, such as two cars crossing each other as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:event}. 
From this event, the timestamp 
of the event in both videos was found and the difference between them was calculated 
to obtain the temporal offset of 2.6 seconds. After this step, the videos were synchronized by shifting 
one of the videos by this temporal offset and applying the same frame matching algorithm
as before with the new offset. This resulted in two videos that were temporally aligned 
with a maximum temporal frame pairing offset of 55 ms across 15 minute videos.

\paragraph{Spatial Misalignment}
After temporal alignment, spatial alignment between the two modalities was
addressed. The spatial misalignment originates from three independent 
factors, which are the physical separation of the sensors causing parallax, 
the differing fields of view (FOV) of the cameras, and a misalignment in 
the optical axes of the two cameras called boresight error.
The camera parameters used to compute the required corrections are 
listed in Table~\ref{tab:camera_params}.

\paragraph{FOV error}

The thermal camera covers a smaller angular region of the scene
due to its narrower FOV (Table~\ref{tab:camera_params}).
To quantify this, separate horizontal and vertical scale factors
$s_H$ and $s_V$ are defined as the ratio of the real-world
extents $H$ captured by each camera at equal depth $Z$.
The pinhole model is used to derive this ratio. It approximates
the camera projection by treating the lens as a single point,
thereby neglecting distortion at the edges of the image, as
illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:pinhole_fov_scale}. Under this
model, the half-extent at depth $Z$ is $Z\tan(\mathrm{FOV}/2)$.
Since both cameras observe the same depth $Z$, it cancels in
the ratio and both scale factors reduce to the depth-independent
tangent ratio of the FOVs:

\begin{align}
  s_H &= \frac{H_{thm}^H}
              {H_{vis}^H}
       = \frac{Z\tan(\mathrm{FOV_{thm}^H}/2)}
              {Z\tan(\mathrm{FOV_{vis}^H}/2)}
       = \frac{\tan(\mathrm{FOV_{thm}^H}/2)}
              {\tan(\mathrm{FOV_{vis}^H}/2)}
       = \frac{\tan(22.5^\circ)}{\tan(31.85^\circ)}
       = 0.667 \\[6pt]
  s_V &= \frac{H_{thm}^V}
              {H_{vis}^V}
       = \frac{Z\tan(\mathrm{FOV_{thm}^V}/2)}
              {Z\tan(\mathrm{FOV_{vis}^V}/2)}
       = \frac{\tan(\mathrm{FOV_{thm}^V}/2)}
              {\tan(\mathrm{FOV_{vis}^V}/2)}
       = \frac{\tan(18.5^\circ)}{\tan(19.30^\circ)}
       = 0.955
\end{align}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{images/pinhole_fov_scale.png}
    \vspace{-1em}
    \caption{
        Illustration of pinhole model for FOV scaling in one dimension.
    }
    \label{fig:pinhole_fov_scale}
\end{figure}

The thermal image therefore covers 66.7\,\% of the visible frame
horizontally and 95.6\,\% vertically. Multiplying these fractions
by the visible resolution gives the target size of the thermal
content on the visible canvas: $1280 \times 0.667 = 853.5$\,px
wide and $720 \times 0.955 = 688.0$\,px tall. Stretching the
thermal frame to this target size yields pixel-grid scale factors
of $\mathrm{scale}_x = 853.5/640 = 1.334$ and
$\mathrm{scale}_y = 688.0/512 = 1.344$. Assuming a shared
optical axis, the scaled thermal region is then centred on the
visible canvas, giving translations
$t_x = (1280 - 853.5)/2 = 213.3$\,px and
$t_y = (720 - 688.0)/2 = 16.0$\,px. These four values are
combined into a single affine transformation, which is
applied to the thermal image to align it to the visible frame:

\begin{equation}
  M =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1.3335 & 0      & 213.3 \\
    0      & 1.3436 &  16.0
  \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Boresight Error}

After FOV-based alignment, a residual constant pixel offset persists 
across all scene depths. This indicates a boresight error, an angular 
misalignment between the optical axes of the two cameras.
Such misalignment introduces a constant angular offset in the observed 
scene, which manifests as a constant pixel offset in the images regardless 
of object depth. The offset is depth-independent because each pixel subtends 
a fixed solid angle. As distance increases, the physical separation between 
pixel rays grows at the same rate as the inter-camera displacement of an 
object caused by boresight offset. Therefore, the displacement measured 
in pixels remains constant. Consequently, a boresight error is equivalent 
to a uniform shift in pixel space.

The pixel offsets were empirically measured as $\Delta x = -30$\,px and
$\Delta y = -10$\,px. These are added directly to the centering
translation, yielding the corrected affine transformation:

\begin{equation}
  M =
  \begin{pmatrix}
    1.3335 & 0      & 183.3 \\
    0      & 1.3436 &   6.0
  \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}

From the empirical pixel offsets, the corresponding angular misalignment can be estimated using the 
pinhole camera model. The focal length in pixels is derived from the FOV and resolution as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:boresight_misalignment_approximation}a:
%
\begin{equation}
  f_{\mathrm{px}} = \frac{\text{resolution}/2}{\tan(\text{FOV}/2)}
\end{equation}
%
Evaluating for each axis gives $f_{\mathrm{vis},H} = 1030.2$\,px and $f_{\mathrm{vis},V} = 1028.6$\,px.
The angular offsets then follow from the pixel offset and focal length as illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:boresight_misalignment_approximation}b, which can lie well
within typical manufacturing tolerances for camera alignment:

\begin{equation}
  \theta_H = \arctan\!\left(\tfrac{30}{1030.2}\right) = 1.67^\circ, \qquad
  \theta_V = \arctan\!\left(\tfrac{10}{1028.6}\right) = 0.56^\circ
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{images/boresight_misalignment_approximation.png}
    \vspace{-1em}
    \caption{
        Illustration of the pinhole model for boresight angle approximation.
        (a) Focal length in pixels derived from FOV and resolution.
        (b) Angular misalignment derived from pixel offset and focal length.
    }
    \label{fig:boresight_misalignment_approximation}
\end{figure}


\paragraph{Parallax Error}

Even after applying the corrected affine transformation, a residual 
misalignment remains, especially for objects that are close to the 
camera. This effect is known as parallax. Parallax occurs 
because the two camera lenses are physically separated by a small 
distance, the baseline $b$. As a result, each lens observes the same 
scene from a slightly different viewpoint. While distant objects 
appear almost identical in both views, nearby objects shift noticeably 
between them due to this change in perspective.
The resulting pixel shift depends on both the baseline $b$ and the 
distance to the observed object $d$. As illustrated in 
Figure~\ref{fig:parallax_error}, the shift can be approximated as:

\begin{equation}
\Delta\mathrm{px} = \tan(\theta) \times f_{\mathrm{vis,px}}
= \frac{b}{d} \times f_{\mathrm{vis,px}}
\label{eq:parallax_shift}
\end{equation}.
\cite[pp.~701]{torralba2024foundationsComputerVision}

This relationship shows that the pixel shift decreases with 
increasing distance $d$, and approaches zero as $d \to \infty$. 
Consequently, a constant boresight correction provides a good 
approximation for distant scenes, but becomes increasingly 
inaccurate for nearby objects. For the drone application considered 
here, the camera baseline is $b = 0.10$,m and the operating distance 
is about $d = 25$,m. Substituting these values into 
Equation~\ref{eq:parallax_shift} yields a maximum parallax shift 
of $4.12~\text{px}$.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{images/parallax_shift.png}
    \vspace{-1em}
    \caption{
        Illustration of the pinhole camera model used to approximate
        the parallax-induced pixel shift $\Delta\mathrm{px}$.
    }
    \label{fig:parallax_error}
\end{figure}

Although the maximum parallax error this is relatively small, it 
is not negligible. Accurately correcting parallax across 
a scene would require depth information for each pixel, which can 
only be obtained using a depth sensor or stereo reconstruction. 
Such corrections cannot be achieved with a single global affine 
transformation. For this reason, the residual error is considered 
acceptable for the current application and is ignored. However, it 
should be taken into account if the drone operates at significantly 
shorter distances in the future.

Figure~\ref{fig:camera_alignment_result} shows qualitative results of the 
final RGB--thermal alignment. The alignment is not perfect but 
provides a good approximation across the scene. In particular, 
Figure~\ref{fig:camera_alignment_result}(a) illustrates the parallax effect 
for nearby objects. In contrast, 
distant objects, such as the vehicles in (b), are well aligned. 
Minor residual errors can still be observed as shown in (c), which are 
mainly attributed to drone oscillations combined with slight differences in 
frame capture times, as well as differing camera-dependent distortions 
near the image edges visible for the vehicle near the top.

        (b) Accurate vehicle alignment.

Image alignment is one of the issues that should be considered in cross-modal
image applications. Existing methods [11,16,40,29,6,38,39] usually assume that
visible-infrared image pairs are perfectly geometrically aligned, and they directly
perform the multi-modal fusion methods. 
Actually, after the image registration
algorithm, the paired images are just weakly aligned (shown in Fig. 1(a)). However, 
objects differ not only
in position, but also in scale and angle. These three deviations (position, size and
angle) of the paired arbitrary oriented objects are closely coupled, changing one
will affect another one, which makes the alignment operation more complicated.
As shown in Fig. 1(b), the dashed boxes represent the position of the corresponding 
bounding-boxes in the infrared images. We find that the same object
on image pairs differs in location, scale and angle. Therefore, weakly misalignment 
in cross-modal aerial images is a common issue that needs to be addressed.
To demonstrate the generality of the misalignment 
problem in aerial
imagery, we also obtain the statistics information of DroneVehicle dataset,
a dataset that provides RGBT data taken from the UAV drone perspective. we
separately count the number of bounding-boxes with deviations (position and
size offset by 3 pixels, angle offset by 3 degrees) in the DroneVehicle dataset.
More than 20\% of the bounding-boxes have the misalignment 
problem.
\cite{yuan2022crossModalAlignmentVehicleDetection}

Spatial misalignment: Since RGB and infrared sensors have different coordinate systems,
fields of view, and sampling frequencies, pairs of RGB and infrared images usually are spatial
misaligned [21], resulting in low-quality bounding boxes predicted by RGB and infrared fusion
object detection, as shown in Figure 1a. Figure 1b indicates that the fusion of original RGB
and infrared images via the image fusion algorithm [22] will take in a significantly misaligned
ghost, which will also disturb localization. The downsampling may decrease the degree of
misalignment [8], yet the situation still exists, as shown in Figure 1c. The performance of
RGB and infrared fusion detection methods that directly fuse misaligned features is not ideal.
Therefore, achieving spatial alignment between RGB and infrared modalities is crucial. The
training process of existing RGB and infrared fusion object-detection networks [7,19] relies on
manually aligned datasets in advance. However, manual alignment is a labor-intensive task
[13,23], and there will still be subtle misalignments after manual processing, such as the FLIR
dataset [24]. A more feasible way is to take misaligned RGB and infrared images as input
directly, and how to adaptively perform spatial alignment at the feature level is the concern of
our work. Furthermore, RGB and infrared images usually have different resolutions. Existing
works [22,25] directly resize different modal images to the same size through downscaling or
upscaling, either dropping beneficial information or introducing redundancy and increasing
computational overhead.
\cite{he2023misalignedRGBTadaptiveCalibration}
