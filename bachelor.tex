\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

\begin{otherlanguage*}{english}
Fixed ground-level traffic sensors provide limited spatial coverage, and extension to rural
or mountainous terrain is costly. Unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) swarms can cover large road
segments continuously at lower deployment cost. Reliable vehicle detection across the full
outdoor operating range is therefore required. Visible-spectrum cameras fail in low light and
adverse weather. Thermal infrared cameras capture heat emitted by vehicles independently of
ambient illumination, and a combined RGB and LWIR sensor system thus extends detection
robustness beyond single-modality approaches.

This thesis presents a combined RGB-thermal vehicle detection and tracking pipeline for the
3D-Net UAV traffic monitoring project. The system runs on a Raspberry Pi~5 paired with a
Hailo-8 neural processing unit, mounted on a twinFOLD KAT hexacopter operating alongside
road segments at low altitude. Seven fusion architectures are trained on a pool of four 
datasets comprising 61,055 paired images, including
the newly collected 3D-Net dataset, a low-altitude oblique RGB-LWIR benchmark with 12,263
image pairs. All models are evaluated at full precision and after INT8 quantization on the
Hailo-8 accelerator.

All compiled fusion architectures exceed the 20\,fps camera frame rate on the Hailo-8 at
approximately 5\,W system power draw, under 0.3\,\% of total UAV power. Among the compiled
architectures, late fusion provides the most favorable balance of detection accuracy
and quantization robustness. Fusion models improve over the RGB-only
baseline by 5 to 7 percent mAP50 under adverse weather and up to 9 percent at night. INT8
quantization introduces a 0.1 to 5 percent mAP50 reduction for compiled fusion models.
ByteTrack is selected as the tracker and achieves a HOTA of 0.946 at 0.36\,ms per frame on
the Raspberry Pi~5 CPU. The end-to-end pipeline reaches a HOTA of 0.747 on the test set.

To the best of our knowledge, this work presents the first systematic comparison of multiple
RGB-LWIR fusion architectures under INT8 quantization on a dedicated edge accelerator for
aerial vehicle traffic monitoring.
\end{otherlanguage*}





\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

Traffic systems face growing pressure from increasing vehicle volumes
and rising congestion frequency. Fixed ground-level sensors, including induction loops, roadside cameras, and
radar detectors, provide the foundation of most current monitoring
infrastructures. Their spatial coverage is constrained by installation density,
and their deployment costs are high. As a result, these systems struggle to
adapt to dynamic or spatially extended congestion events. Probe vehicle data
from GPS-equipped vehicles extends coverage to unmonitored segments at lower
deployment cost. However, it only captures connected vehicles, provides no
vehicle-level classification, and offers no visual information for incident
detection. Neither approach is therefore suited to fine-grained,
real-time traffic analysis, particularly in rural or mountain areas where
fixed infrastructure is impractical.

Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) offer a practical path toward
flexible, real-time aerial traffic monitoring. Compared to traditional
fixed-ground sensors, UAV technologies provide greater mobility, higher
spatial and temporal resolution, more flexible deployment, and richer
data~\cite{liu2025skysEyePerspectiveUAVHighwayReview}. A coordinated group of aerial
platforms can therefore cover large road segments continuously without the spatial
constraints of fixed installations. Outdoor aerial operation, however, exposes
the perception system to variable illumination and adverse weather conditions
that visible-spectrum cameras alone cannot reliably handle.

This thesis addresses the perception task in this UAV monitoring system of reliable vehicle
tracking across the full range of outdoor operating conditions.
Visible-spectrum perception performs well under clear daytime conditions but
degrades in low light or adverse weather. The thermal infrared modality records heat
emitted by vehicles independently of ambient light and can therefore retain
tracking capability when visible imaging fails. A combined RGB and thermal
sensor system is therefore used to increase tracking robustness in uncontrolled
outdoor environments. Top-down operation above active roadways is restricted
in Austria and several other jurisdictions, so the UAV flies alongside rural
road segments rather than hovering above them. The combined sensor data is
processed onboard the UAV rather than being streamed to a ground station.
This reduces end-to-end latency, relaxes demands on the communication link,
and complies with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). UAV payload
and power budgets, however, impose strict constraints on the onboard hardware.
Fusing two modalities through a deep neural network places additional demands
on the inference pipeline within these constraints.

The thesis introduces a low-altitude oblique paired RGB-LWIR dataset and
evaluates seven fusion architectures spanning early, mid, late,
and score fusion strategies on the self-collected and public datasets.
The best-performing architecture
is deployed on a Hailo-8 accelerator under INT8 quantization. To the best of
our knowledge, no prior work reports a multi-architecture RGB-LWIR fusion
comparison on a dedicated edge accelerator under a low-altitude oblique
viewpoint for vehicle traffic monitoring.

Two research questions guide this thesis. The first asks whether reliable
vehicle detection from aerial imagery is achievable in real-time on a UAV edge
device equipped with RGB and thermal sensors. The second asks what impact
different sensor fusion strategies have on detection performance in this
operational context.

The remainder of this thesis is structured as follows.
Chapter~\ref{chap:literature_review} reviews related work on UAV-based
traffic monitoring, sensor fusion, and aerial object detection.
Chapter~\ref{chap:hardware} describes and justifies the hardware components used in this
work, including the UAV platform, the onboard processing unit, and the
multi-sensor system.
Chapter~\ref{chap:dataset} presents the dataset collected for this work and
the public datasets incorporated alongside it.
Chapter~\ref{chap:object_detection} introduces machine learning object
detection methods and deep multi-sensor fusion techniques.
Chapter~\ref{chap:tracking} introduces multi-object tracking methods and
reports their evaluation for this application.
Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation} details the development and implementation
of the detection model, including the training pipeline, the compilation to
the edge device, and the final vehicle tracking service running on the UAV.
Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} evaluates the proposed approach, followed by
Chapter~\ref{chap:limitations}, which discusses its limitations.
Finally, Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} summarizes the findings and outlines
directions for future work.

This thesis is conducted within the 3D-Net research project. The project
investigates UAV swarms and next-generation communication technologies for
traffic flow monitoring in support of the mobility transition toward sustainable
transportation. It is funded by the Bundesministerium für Innovation,
Mobilität und Infrastruktur (BMIMI) and the Österreichische
Forschungsförderungsgesellschaft (FFG). Project partners include Twins GmbH,
which contributes UAV hardware and test flight operations, and Lakeside Labs,
which develops drone swarm coordination. Bernard Gruppe GmbH is responsible
for online traffic data analysis and object detection. The present thesis
addresses the vehicle detection component of this project.

\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{chap:literature_review}

UAV-based traffic monitoring has matured into a recognised research area
over the past decade
\cite{barmpounakis2016UAVtransportEngineeringSurvey,outay2020UAVroadSafetyTrafficReview,liu2025skysEyePerspectiveUAVHighwayReview}.
One of the most influential large-scale field deployment to date is the pNEUMA
experiment of
\citet{barmpounakis2020pNEUMAdroneFieldExperiment}, in which ten drones
hovered in coordinated formation over a 1.3\,km$^2$ urban area in central
Athens and recorded the trajectories of half a million vehicles
across 100 intersections. Follow-on work has extended the same
dataset to visual swarm-level anomaly detection
\cite{kim2023visualExtensionsAnomalyDetectionSwarmDrones}. Despite its scale, pNEUMA exhibits imprecise bounding boxes and trajectory
anomalies attributed to suboptimal computer-vision tools.
\citet{fonod2025advancedComputerVisionVehicleTrajectoriesDroneImagery} develop
Geo-trax, a computer vision framework for high-accuracy trajectory extraction
from drone imagery. They also release the Songdo Traffic and Songdo Vision
datasets to address these gaps. The framework extracts georeferenced vehicle
trajectories from high-resolution drone imagery captured in Songdo at roughly
150\,m altitude with a top-down view. It stabilises the resulting tracks
through orthophoto-based image registration in post-flight processing.
\citet{espadaler2025accurateSafetyCongestionMonitoringFrameworkSwarmDrones}
employs this framework to obtain vehicle trajectories at signalised intersections
and applies surrogate safety measures such as Time-To-Collision to relate observed speeds to the spatial
pattern of unsafe events. Both works rely on visible-spectrum video alone and process the
captured footage offline after flight. One layer above perception,
\citet{maljkovic2025learningBasedTrafficMonitoringSwarmDrones} propose an
online semi-decentralised reinforcement-learning framework that schedules
monitoring trajectories for a swarm of low-cost drones. However, the method assumes a city-wide real-time congestion signal is
already available.

Beyond these comprehensive systems, separate deployed work
follows the same stationary, predominantly top-down pattern
for individual road segments and intersections. Signalised and unsignalised
intersections as well as roundabouts have been monitored from a hovering
drone for traffic flow estimation
\cite{khan2018UAVTrafficSignalisedIntersections, khan2018UAVtrafficUrbanRoundabouts} and
safety analysis
\cite{yang2021functionalSafetyRiskSignalisedIntersections,
    vuong2021intersectionMixedTrafficSafetyCloudModel}.
In addition, lane-change risk has been examined from drone footage of interchange
merging areas and freeway segments
\cite{gu2019UAVcrashRiskInterchangeMerging,park2018laneChangeRiskTrajectoryData}.
The DARTS system of \citet{li2025DARTSdroneTrafficIncidentDetection} explored
incident detection on an interstate highway with real-time inference through onboard
thermal imaging. They report 99\,\% accuracy for incident detection on their self-collected dataset
with a successful incident detection during an interstate field test.

Four limitations recur across the deployed systems that target vehicles.
First, perception relies on a single modality, either RGB or thermal,
which leaves operational gaps under low-light or adverse weather
conditions. Second, the viewpoint is predominantly top-down. This
operating mode is restricted in several jurisdictions including Austria,
where UAV flights directly above active road segments are not generally
permitted. Third, the platform typically hovers in place above an
intersection or short segment. Such configurations suit microscopic
intersection studies but do not extend to sparse rural or
alpine corridors where UAV flights enable more ground coverage than fixed
ground sensors. Fourth, the processing pipeline either streams raw
video to a ground server or analyses footage after flight.
Streaming requires a stable high-bandwidth radio link that is unreliable
in mountainous terrain, and it also raises GDPR concerns because
identifiable imagery leaves the platform. Post-flight processing in turn
rules out real-time response. Together these constraints define the
design space that the present work targets.

The single-modality limitation of the deployed systems surveyed above
motivates paired RGB-thermal sensing as the natural next step.
Paired RGB-thermal benchmarks now span vehicle
detection~\cite{suo2023hitUAVDataset,sun2022droneVehicleRGBThermalUAVDataset},
multi-object tracking~\cite{zhang2022VTUAVDataset,nie2025m3ot}, and nighttime
conditions~\cite{zhang2024trafficNightDataset,feng2023rtdodDataset}. These
benchmarks have stimulated a parallel line of fusion-method research. Modern
RGB-thermal detectors report substantial detection gains over single-modality
baselines~\cite{qingyun2021CFT,yuan2024c2former,guo2024damsdet,wan2025yolov11rgbt,li2025DEPFusionUAVMultispectralObjectDetection}.
Real-time paired RGB-thermal inference has been demonstrated onboard flying
platforms outside the traffic
domain~\cite{speth2022deepLearningRGBThermalOnboardDrone,bultmann2021realTimeMultiModalSemanticFusion},
but no prior work demonstrates this capability for vehicle traffic monitoring.
The fusion approaches explored in those systems have mostly been limited to separate
per-modality detectors combined as an ensemble. Quantization-aware RGB-thermal fusion remains largely
unexplored. To the best of our knowledge,
\citet{kafka2025EarlyFusionQuantizedModels} is the only published study on
quantized RGB-thermal fusion for UAV edge deployment, and it only addresses a
single fusion architecture.

\section{UAV Platform}
\vspace{-0.5em}

The 3D-Net project envisions a coordinated fleet of four UAVs with distinct operational roles
and automated battery-based replacement to sustain continuous road coverage.
All test flights for object tracking and data collection reported in this thesis
were conducted with a single unit under manual control.
Future project stages will introduce smaller, purpose-built platforms with extended flight times
and autonomous coordination, but these developments lie outside the scope of this work.

The aerial platform used in this work is the
\textit{twinFOLD KAT}, a heavy-payload hexacopter developed
and provided by
twins GmbH. According to the official product specification
document \cite{twinsKATspec2021}, the system has a maximum takeoff
weight of 12\,kg and supports a payload of up to 5.5\,kg
including batteries and sensors.

The UAV achieves a nominal flight endurance of 35
minutes, with maximum flight times of up to 45 minutes under
favorable conditions. The maximum horizontal flight speed is
specified as 7\,m/s. Furthermore, the system is certified for
operation in light rain or snowfall and supports missions up
to 3000\,m above mean sea level, which allows for flights in
all tyrolian areas.

\section{Onboard Processing Unit}
\vspace{-0.5em}

Onboard data processing requires a platform capable of
real-time multimodal inference under strict weight, size, and power
constraints imposed by the UAV, while also matching
organizational experience and existing infrastructure.
Three candidate platforms were evaluated, namely the
\textit{NVIDIA Jetson Nano}, the \textit{NVIDIA Jetson Xavier NX},
and a \textit{Raspberry Pi 5} paired with a \textit{Hailo-8}
AI accelerator, all of which are actively used within the
organization.

Both NVIDIA Jetson platforms natively support standard
inference frameworks such as ONNX Runtime and TensorRT
without hardware- or model-specific compilation steps. The
entry-level \textit{Jetson Nano} delivers 512\,GFLOPS of AI
throughput with 4\,GB of RAM at a peak TDP of 10\,W~\cite{jetsonNanoComputeModuleDatasheet2024},
supporting FP32 and FP16 but lacking GPU hardware for INT8 inference,
which proves limiting for real-time processing of fused RGB
and thermal detection pipelines. The \textit{Jetson Xavier
    NX} provides substantially higher
compute at 21\,TOPS and adds INT8 hardware support with 8\,GB of RAM but at 15\,W~\cite{jetsonXavierNXDatasheet2020}.
As systems-on-module, both Jetson variants require a
separate carrier board, heatsink, and fan assembly for deployment, bringing
the developer kit dimensions and weights to
100$\times$79$\times$29\,mm at 141\,g for the Nano and
130$\times$90$\times$34\,mm at 172\,g for the Xavier
NX~\cite{jetsonNanoDeveloperKitWeight2019,jetsonXavierNXDevkitWeight,jetsonNanoDeveloperKitSpecification2020,jetsonXavierNXDevKitSpecification2020}.
This footprint presents an integration challenge for small
rotary-wing UAVs with limited payload budgets. The third candidate, a
\textit{Raspberry Pi 5} paired with a \textit{Hailo-8} AI
accelerator achieves 26\,TOPS of dedicated INT8 neural network
inference, measures 86$\times$58$\times$26\,mm and weighs 89\,g at a peak
power consumption of
8.65\,W with an average power consumption during active inference of 7\,W
as found by \citet{pereira2025PerformanceRaspberryPiAIHAT}.~\cite{raspberryPi5datasheet2026,hailo8_m2datasheet2021,hailo8_productBrief2023}
The Hailo-8 attaches directly via the Raspberry Pi 5's
native PCIe 2.0 interface through an M.2 HAT\texttt{+}
carrier board, while the Raspberry Pi 5 itself provides up to 8\,GB of LPDDR4X
RAM~\cite{raspberryPi5datasheet2026}.
Table~\ref{tab:opu_comparison} summarises these platforms
quantitatively across the metrics most relevant to UAV
deployment.

One trade-off of the Hailo ecosystem is that models must be
compiled and quantized using the Hailo Dataflow Compiler as ,
introducing longer iteration cycles during development as the
quantization process requires 21 hours on the GPU workers employed
with Nvidia 2080Ti GPUs.
In practice, however, quantization is equally necessary on
the Jetson platforms to achieve competitive throughput.
\citet{filippou2025edge} show that FP16 quantization of
YOLOv8 yields 1.2$\times$ faster inference with a
mAP50-95 drop below 0.5\,\%. Quantization is therefore a
shared requirement across all platforms.

No literature was found that benchmarks all three platforms
against each other directly on computer vision tasks.
However, \citet{filippou2025edge} compare the Hailo-8 to the Jetson Nano
and report a 4.58$\times$ throughput improvement for the Hailo-8 on the
YOLOv8n model with 2 million parameters
and a 3.42$\times$ advantage on the larger YOLOv8s model with 9 million
parameters~\cite{yaseen2024yolov8}.
For the Jetson Xavier NX relative to the Jetson Nano,
\citet{feng2022benchmarkPerformanceYoloEdgeDevices} report a
3.65$\times$ throughput gain on the YOLOv3-tiny model with 8.8 million
parameters~\cite{li2020systematicStudyTinyYOLO3Inference}.
In both studies, the Jetson measurements were obtained using Float16
quantization and batch sizes of 1 were used to simulate realtime
performance. Since INT8 quantization can yield further throughput gains
over FP16 depending on the GPU architecture, the Xavier NX figures may
underestimate its true advantage
over the Nano, as INT8 inference time was not tested.
Although the two studies use different YOLO versions, the YOLOv8s and
YOLOv3-tiny models share a similar parameter count, making the
comparisons roughly compatible.
\citet{lema2024quantitativeComparisonObjectDetectionEdgeDevices} further
show that inference time scales roughly linearly with the number of model
parameters across multiple YOLO versions and platforms including the
Jetson Nano, which makes parameter count is the dominant factor even when
architectures differ.
These empirical results are also consistent with the published
operations-per-second specifications of the respective platforms.

Based on this comparison, the Raspberry Pi 5 with Hailo-8 was selected
over the Jetson alternatives. It achieves throughput comparable to the
Jetson Xavier NX while being lighter and more power-efficient.
Although the heavy-payload twinFOLD KAT used in this project is not
constrained by these margins, future production deployments on smaller
UAVs with limited payload capacity would benefit from the reduced weight
and power draw, as smaller platforms are both cheaper and easier to
operate under current regulatory frameworks.

To facilitate secure attachment of the processing unit to
the UAV airframe, a custom bracket was designed and
fabricated using fused deposition modelling (FDM) 3D
printing, mounting the Raspberry Pi 5 together with the
M.2 HAT\texttt{+} Hailo-8 module on the UAV as seen in
Figure~\ref{fig:raspberryPiBracket}.

\vspace{-2.5em}
\section{Sensor System}
\vspace{-0.5em}

The choice of the sensor system was primarily driven by the
selection of suitable data modalities for reliable vehicle
detection in aerial imagery and ease of integration.
The most widely used modalities in autonomous automotive
applications are being evaluated: RGB imaging, infrared
imaging, LiDAR, and radar. Each modality offers
distinct advantages and limitations in terms of spatial
resolution, robustness to illumination changes, weather
tolerance, geometric information content, and computational
requirements. After modality evaluation, the selected
hardware \textit{Workswell WIRIS Enterprise} is described,
including specifications and the selection rationale.

\vspace{-1em}
\subsection{Remote Sensing}

Remote sensing in the context of UAV-based vehicle detection is
the acquisition of information about the Earth's surface and objects on it
without making physical contact. The remotely collected data can
be of many different types, yet the primary sensing approach in
autonomous systems and object detection applications is
based on the detection of electromagnetic radiation.
\cite[p.~1]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}

Figure~\ref{fig:electromagnetic_remote_sensing} illustrates
the elements involved in electromagnetic remote sensing of earth
resources in airborne systems.  The elements of the data
acquisition process are energy sources (a), propagation of energy
through the atmosphere (b), energy interactions with earth surface
features (c), retransmission of energy through the atmosphere (d),
and sensors (e), which results in the generation
of sensor data in pictorial form.
\cite[p.~2]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}
Note that passive systems rely on external sources of energy, such
as reflected sunlight as in photography, or heat emitted by objects
themselves as in thermal imaging, while active systems such as
radar and LiDAR transmit their own energy and record the return signal
\cite[p.~9,45]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{images/electromagnetic_remote_sensing_modified.png}
    \caption{
        Schematic representation of the generalized processes and elements involved in
        electromagnetic remote sensing. Modified after
        \cite[p.~16]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}
    }
    \label{fig:electromagnetic_remote_sensing}
\end{figure}

As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:electromagnetic_spectrum},
the electromagnetic spectrum spans radiation bands
characterized by wavelength. The regions most relevant to remote sensing
are the visible band (\SIrange{0.4}{0.7}{\micro\meter}), where optical
cameras operate; the near-infrared, where LiDAR typically operates at
\SI{1.064}{\micro\meter}; the longwave infrared (LWIR,
8--\SI{14}{\micro\meter}), used by thermal sensors; and the microwave
region (\SI{1}{\milli\meter} to \SI{1}{\meter}), where radar operates.
\cite[pp.~4]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/electromagnetic_spectrum.png}
    \caption{
        The electromagnetic spectrum. The visible spectrum is
        highlighted, and other important regions are indicated.
        \cite[p.~5]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}
    }
    \label{fig:electromagnetic_spectrum}
\end{figure}


Shorter wavelengths provide higher spatial detail and fine-grained information like texture and color, but are more
susceptible to atmospheric absorption and scattering by fog, rain, and aerosols.
In contrast, longer wavelengths penetrate adverse weather more effectively but
provide lower spatial resolution due to their lower energy content
and the larger areas they must view in consequence to obtain a detectable signal.
\cite[pp.~6, 10, 386, 481]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}

In the following, the available electromagnetic remote sensing modalities
are evaluated against the specific requirements of UAV-based vehicle
detection. The evaluation covers how each modality captures different
aspects of the environment and how its performance varies under
different environmental conditions.

\vspace{-0.6em}
\paragraph{Optical Imaging}

RGB cameras are passive sensors that capture visible light reflected
from scene objects. A lens projects this light onto a two-dimensional
array of photosensitive detectors, where each detector is sensitive to
one of three spectral bands (red, green, or blue). The recorded
brightness values across all three bands are combined to produce a
color image.
\citetext{\citealp[pp.~66, 81, 93]{szeliski2022computerVision}; \citealp[pp.~89, 115]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}; \citealp{agrawal2019comparativeRemoteSensing}}

Cameras are the most widely used remote sensing modality in autonomous
traffic applications for good reason. RGB cameras offer exceptionally
high spatial resolution and capture rich semantic information, including
color, texture, shape, and fine-grained patterns over distances of several
hundred meters \cite{wu2025comparativeAnalysisLiDARRadarCamera}. This
detailed visual content enables tasks such as reading road signs, identifying
lane markings, and recognizing traffic states, many of which are fundamentally
inaccessible to purely geometric sensors or sensors operating outside the
visible spectrum.
Their low cost, small form factor, and low energy consumption
have made them a widely adopted sensing modality in autonomous
traffic applications.
Their ubiquity has been further amplified by rapid advances in deep learning
and convolutional neural networks, which have significantly improved scene
object detection performance.
\cite{soltanirad2025autonomousTransportationPerception,bustos2023reviewObjectDetectionIR}

Despite these strengths, reliance on visual-spectrum imaging alone is
insufficient for robust perception. Cameras, although equipped with
exposure and gain controls to adapt to varying illumination, remain
passive sensors and are therefore fundamentally dependent on ambient
light. In low-light conditions, such as nighttime, their ability to
capture clear and detailed imagery degrades substantially, leading to
reduced contrast and diminished detection performance. While vehicle
headlights partially compensate by illuminating the roadway ahead, they
provide limited illumination of the vehicle itself, restricting their
usefulness for vision-based vehicle detection. Moreover, headlights are
not a reliable discriminative feature. Many objects emit or reflect light,
headlights may be occluded from certain viewpoints, particularly in
side-view observations, or may not be active at all.

In addition, adverse weather conditions, including fog, rain, and snow,
further degrade image quality by scattering and absorbing light during
atmospheric transmission. High dynamic range scenes, such as environments
with direct sunlight or deep shadows, also present challenges,
as cameras often struggle to simultaneously preserve details in both
brightly illuminated and dark regions. Glare and strong backlighting may
lead to overexposure and localized loss of detail. Moreover, mirror
reflections and lens contamination caused by dirt or moisture
hinder reliable vehicle detection. Examples of these conditions are
illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:camera_lighting_defects}.
To address these limitations, alternative remote sensing modalities
aimed at enabling more robust vehicle detection beyond the constraints
of the visible spectrum are explored in the following.
\cite{kenk2020dawn,yahiaoui2020sunGlareDetection,jin2026rasmd,jia2021llvip}

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/camera_lighting_defects_collection.png}
    \caption{
        Collection of lighting conditions and environmental factors that
        negatively impact vehicle detection in camera images.
        ~\cite{kenk2020dawn, yahiaoui2020sunGlareDetection, jin2026rasmd, jia2021llvip}
    }
    \label{fig:camera_lighting_defects}

\paragraph{Infrared Imaging}

Infrared imaging is a passive remote sensing modality that captures
electromagnetic radiation in the infrared spectrum, just
beyond the visible range. Unlike optical imaging, which relies
on reflected sunlight or artificial illumination, infrared sensors
detect radiation emitted by objects themselves. This independence 
from external illumination enables day and night detection.
Infrared sensor optics follow the same principles as optical cameras, 
but the detector array responds to the longer wavelengths.
\cite[pp.~243]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}

Blackbody radiation is central to infrared imaging.
All objects emit thermal radiation as a function of their temperature, and the
blackbody model describes an ideal radiator that fully absorbs
and reemits all incident energy.
As Figure~\ref{fig:black_body_radiation} shows, both emitted energy and spectral distribution vary with temperature. Higher temperatures produce greater
total radiation and shift the peak toward shorter wavelengths, according to Wien's 
displacement law. The sun approximates a 6000~K blackbody, with a spectral
peak near 0.5~$\mu$m, while Earth's surface at ${\sim}300$~K or 27~$^\circ$C peaks at
${\sim}9.7~\mu$m. Consequently, the long-wave infrared
(LWIR) band (8--14~$\mu$m) is of interest for object detection, as it
captures thermal radiation from objects at Earth surface temperatures,
enabling the detection of targets such as vehicles and personnel that exhibit a 
temperature contrast relative to their surroundings.
\cite[pp.~7,~218]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{images/spectral_distribution_temperatures_lillesand_2015_remote_sensing.png}
    \caption{
        Spectral distribution of energy radiated from blackbodies
        of various temperatures.
        \cite[p.~8]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}
    }
    \label{fig:black_body_radiation}
\end{figure}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:vehicle_thermal_radiation}, thermographic measurements
of the Volkswagen e-Golf and Golf 1.5TSI after 30 minutes of city driving
at 5$^\circ$C and $-1~^\circ$C reveal characteristic heat patterns across
both vehicles. The combustion engine's hood exhibits a pronounced temperature
increase, while the electric vehicle's front surface remains near ambient
temperature. Tire, wheel arch, headlight, and window signatures provide 
detectable thermal patterns for infrared-based vehicle detection.
\cite{svorc2020detectionElectricVehicleThermal}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/vehicle_thermal_radiation_tichy_2021_thermal_vehicle_feature_detection.png}
    \caption{
        Example of thermal measurements of the \textit{Volkswagen e-Golf} and
        \textit{Golf 1.5 TSI} after 30 minutes of city driving. Modified after
        \cite{svorc2020detectionElectricVehicleThermal}
    }
    \label{fig:vehicle_thermal_radiation}
\end{figure}


LWIR imaging overcomes many of the limitations of optical imaging
previously illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:camera_lighting_defects}.
Unlike RGB sensors, LWIR cameras are unaffected by visible illumination,
enabling reliable detection of vehicles and pedestrians in low-light,
nighttime, and foggy conditions. In full darkness, LWIR is the only
passive sensor capable of pedestrian detection. LWIR cameras are
equally immune to glare from direct sunlight, vehicle headlights,
reflections, and high-contrast backlighting, all of which cause
overexposure and loss of detail in optical imagery, as illustrated
in Figure~\ref{fig:comparison_recognition_vis_nir_swir_lwir}.
\cite{pinchon2018allWeatherVision}

Compared to NIR and SWIR, LWIR performs better in adverse weather.
In fog combined with oncoming headlights, VIS, NIR, and SWIR sensors
experience glare that renders vehicle recognition impossible, while
LWIR maintains consistent detection regardless of headlight state.
Due to its longer wavelength, LWIR is less susceptible to
atmospheric scattering from fog, snow, and
sand~\cite{pinchon2018allWeatherVision}. Nevertheless, water vapor
in fog and rain absorbs and scatters infrared radiation, which reduces
thermal contrast and detection range in very dense
fog~\cite{rivera2022analysisThermalExtremeFog}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{images/comparison_recognition_vis_nir_swir_lwir_pinchon2018allWeatherVision.png}
    \caption{
        VIS, NIR, SWIR, and LWIR imagery under adverse conditions of
        headlight glare (top) and fog tunnel (bottom). 
        Modified after~\cite{pinchon2018allWeatherVision}.
    }
    \label{fig:comparison_recognition_vis_nir_swir_lwir}
\end{figure}

Thermal imaging, however, has its own limitations. Compared to RGB sensors,
thermal cameras generally offer lower spatial resolution, as the low energy of 
long-wavelength radiation (Fig.~\ref{fig:black_body_radiation}) requires larger 
sensing areas for a detectable signal. This also results in generally
lower image contrast than in the visible spectrum \cite[p.~6-8]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}.
Thermal images also lack the visual detail, texture, and color information of optical
cameras, all important cues for recognition and classification
\cite{bustos2023reviewObjectDetectionIR}.

\newpage
Environmental conditions further constrain thermal performance by reducing the temperature
difference between objects and their surroundings. Wind cools warm objects and rain absorbs 
their thermal energy, both producing a more uniform background and diminishing contrast
\cite{stlaurent2007combinationColorThermal, bustos2023reviewObjectDetectionIR}.
Solar heating further affects thermal contrast, as it warms objects differently depending
on their thermal inertia. Asphalt and vehicles
heat considerably by day, while water, trees, and people change little~\cite{stlaurent2007combinationColorThermal, choi2018kaist}. 
In summer, this can reduce the temperature contrast between vehicles and background 
as the entire scene heats up. This reduces thermal performance precisely when optical 
cameras perform well under clear-sky conditions.

\vspace{-0.6em}
\paragraph{LiDAR}

LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) is an active remote sensing technique
for range-finding. It projects an optical signal onto a target and detects the
reflected or backscattered return. By processing this return signal, the
system determines the distance to the target and constructs a 3D point cloud
of the surrounding environment, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:lidar_point_cloud}.
LiDAR systems typically employ laser sources in the near-infrared spectral region,
with wavelengths between 0.80 and 1.55\,\textmu m. This range coincides with
an atmospheric transmission window, where water absorption is low. These
wavelengths are also invisible to the human eye, which is an important
safety consideration \cite{royo2019overviewLiDARAutonomousVehicles}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{images/lidar_point_cloud_melotti_2020_multimodal_recognition.png}
    \caption{
        KITTI 2D Object Detection Dataset sample with the 
        3D point cloud projected onto the image and coloured by distance.
        \cite{melotti2020multimodalCameraLiDARRecognition}
    }
    \label{fig:lidar_point_cloud}
\end{figure}


Two factors constrain the maximum measurable object recognition range in LiDAR systems.
The first is atmospheric scattering, which causes the laser pulse to lose
energy as it travels, leaving only a small fraction of emitted light to
return to the detector.
\cite{royo2019overviewLiDARAutonomousVehicles}
This effect is worsened by adverse weather conditions such as fog, snow,
and rain, analogous to the scattering behavior previously described for
near-infrared cameras, as LiDAR operates in the same electromagnetic range.
In such conditions, suspended particles redirect a portion of the emitted
pulse back toward the sensor before it reaches the target, a phenomenon
known as backscatter.
At a visibility of \SI{25}{\meter}, backscatter reduces the perceivable
range to approximately \SI{6}{\meter} and introduces numerous false
measurements, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:fog_chamber_rgb_lidar_radar}.
Therefore, LiDAR is a viable solution for night driving, yet unreliable
under adverse weather conditions.
\cite{bijelic2020seeingThroughFog, kim2023analysisLiDARPerformanceFogRain}


\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/fog_chamber_rgb_lidar_radar_bijelic_2020_seeing_through_fog.png}
    \caption{
        RGB camera, lidar, and radar responses in a fog 
        chamber under clear conditions (top)
        and dense fog at \SI{23}{\meter} visibility (bottom).
        \cite{bijelic2020seeingThroughFog}
    }
    \label{fig:fog_chamber_rgb_lidar_radar}
\end{figure}

The second factor limiting object recognition range is the limited
information content of point clouds. Unlike RGB images, point clouds
lack texture and color information, and their sparsity worsens with
distance, making it difficult to recognize objects at longer ranges.
\cite{wang2023lidarOnly3DObjectDetection, qi2020imvotenet}

\vspace{-0.6em}
\paragraph{Radar}

The basic operating principle of imaging radar (Radio Detection and
Ranging) is largely similar to that of LiDAR systems. While LiDAR
emits laser pulses in the near-infrared spectrum, radar transmits
short microwave pulses and detects the reflected signals using
antennas. The range of objects is estimated from the time delay of
the returned signal, their radial velocity from the Doppler shift,
and their angular position from phase differences across the antenna
array, which allows estimation of both azimuth (horizontal) and
elevation (vertical) angles.
\citetext{\citealp[pp.~389, 400]{lillesand2015remoteSensing}; \citealp{kurniawan2023clusterfusionRadarCamera3DObjectDetection}; \citealp{srivastav2023radarsAutonomousDriving}}

Radar data can be represented in several formats, including the
range-azimuth-Doppler (RAD) tensor, the range-azimuth heatmap, and
point clouds. The RAD tensor captures observations across range,
Doppler, azimuth, and elevation, and allows object position, speed,
and coarse 2D shape to be inferred directly. Due to their large size,
RAD tensors are often compressed into a range-azimuth heatmap, which
forms a 2D radar image in a top-down view by collapsing the Doppler
dimension, as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:range_azimuth_heatmap}. Radar
point clouds consist of sparse 3D data points extracted as amplitude
peaks from the RAD tensor, where each point encodes the 3D position
of the detection and its radial velocity. Point clouds are commonly
represented in a bird's eye view or
in a perspective view from the sensor's viewpoint.
\cite{srivastav2023radarsAutonomousDriving}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/range_azimuth_map_2021_wang_rodnet.png}
    \caption{
        Range-azimuth heatmaps with corresponding RGB images.
        Examples depict two pedestrians merged into a single azimuth 
        peak (left), an occluded vehicle (center), and light-independent detection (right).
        \cite{wang2021rodnetRadarObjectDetection}
    }
    \label{fig:range_azimuth_heatmap}
\end{figure}

Overall, radar sensors provide high resolution information about object distance
and velocity, as well as low-resolution estimates of shape, size, and
material properties, which is beneficial for object detection.
Since radar systems operate using radio waves of roughly \SI{4}{\milli\meter}
rather than visible or
infrared light, they are largely insensitive to lighting conditions
and remain robust in low-visibility environments such as fog, dust,
or moderate rain. Their relatively large wavelength results in minimal
scattering by small particles, allowing radar signals to propagate
through adverse weather conditions with little attenuation, as
illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:signal_attenuation_radar_lidar}.
This also enables radar to detect objects over long distances and
to observe objects under partial occlusion.
\cite{kurniawan2023clusterfusionRadarCamera3DObjectDetection, karangwa2023reviewVehicleDetectionAutonomousDriving, srivastav2023radarsAutonomousDriving}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/signal_attenuation_radar_lidar_2023_srivastav_radars_autonomous_vehicles.png}
    \caption{
        Signal power attenuation of lidar and radar in rain (left) and fog (right).
        Both sensors tolerate heavy rain, fog strongly attenuates lidar.
        \cite{srivastav2023radarsAutonomousDriving}
    }
    \label{fig:signal_attenuation_radar_lidar}
\end{figure}

However, radar sensors provide low angular resolution due to
antenna array size constraints and therefore produce sparse and partial
point clouds of detected objects. This sparsity makes reliable object
recognition difficult when radar data is used alone and often
requires fusion with complementary sensors such as cameras or LiDAR.
This limits the overall weather robustness to that of the fused sensors.
In addition, the large radar wavelength can cause nearby surfaces, such as
the ground or guardrails, to act as specular reflectors, producing spurious
observations in radar data known as clutter that may result in false positives.
\cite{kurniawan2023clusterfusionRadarCamera3DObjectDetection, 
karangwa2023reviewVehicleDetectionAutonomousDriving, 
srivastav2023radarsAutonomousDriving}

\subsection{Sensor Selection}
\label{sec:sensor_selection}

Based on this evaluation, a multimodal configuration combining
RGB and LWIR imaging was selected. RGB cameras provide the most
informative visual features under favorable lighting conditions
among all evaluated modalities.
LWIR imaging was selected as a complementary modality because of
its independence from external illumination and its robustness to
atmospheric scattering at longer wavelengths.
These properties allow LWIR to fill the perception gaps that optical
cameras exhibit in low-light and adverse weather conditions.

LiDAR was excluded because its performance in fog, rain, and snow is
comparable to that of RGB cameras, as shown in
Table~\ref{tab:modality_comparison}.
Its operating spectrum in the near-infrared lies close to the visible
range, which limits its weather robustness advantage over optical sensors.
Radar was also excluded because its low spatial resolution requires
fusion with cameras or LiDAR to achieve reliable object recognition,
which constrains overall weather robustness to that of the fused sensors.
Despite the limitations discussed in the preceding section, the RGB and
LWIR modalities provide a complementary balance of strengths for vehicle
detection under a wide range of conditions.

A notable limitation of this configuration is the absence of active
sensors such as LiDAR or radar, which provide depth information.
Depth data would benefit the traffic congestion classification stage, where
vehicle distance and relative speed are important cues.
A laser-based distance measurement was therefore included in the selected
hardware platform as a partial substitute.
The inclusion of this modality in the object detection pipeline was
considered, with the aim of providing depth information for vehicle
bounding box size estimation.
This approach was rejected because the sensor delivers a single distance
measurement at the optical axis, which may differ considerably from the
positions of detected vehicles and is therefore unsuitable for per-vehicle
size estimation.
The laser-based distance measurement is consequently reserved for the
traffic congestion classification stage.

Based on this specification, sensor data acquisition is performed using the
\textit{Workswell WIRIS Enterprise}, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:WIRIS_Enterprise}.
The WIRIS Enterprise provides RGB imaging at 1920\,×\,1080 pixels,
LWIR imaging at 640\,×\,512 pixels, and a laser-based distance measurement
along the optical axis of up to 1500\,m.
These values fall below the hardware limits of the device and represent
the maximum achievable during simultaneous video recording.
The system is designed for UAV integration and requires only a single
gimbal mount for all three sensors.
This reduces the mechanical complexity of the sensor installation relative
to mounting the sensors separately.
Its weight is specified at less than 680\,g, and its dimensions are
approximately 76\,×\,107\,×\,102\,mm.
The system operates at a supply voltage between 9\,V and 36\,V DC, with
a typical power consumption of 18\,W and a peak of 21\,W.
These power requirements are within the range of standard UAV power
systems~\cite{worksellWIRISspec2022}.


\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{images/wiris_enterprise_shadow.png}
    \caption{
        The Workswell WIRIS Enterprise multi-sensor system.
        \cite{worksellWIRISspec2022}
    }
    \label{fig:WIRIS_Enterprise}
\end{figure}


A primary reason for selecting the \textit{Workswell WIRIS Enterprise}
is the factory-aligned spatial correspondence it provides between the
RGB and LWIR sensors.
RGB and infrared sensors mounted at different physical positions on a
device operate with different coordinate systems and mismatched fields
of view.
They also differ in sampling frequency, which introduces temporal offsets
that cause spatial misalignment for moving objects.
Many existing multi-modal fusion methods assume perfect geometric alignment
between visible-infrared image pairs and fuse features directly.
In practice, however, paired images are only weakly aligned, with objects
differing in position, scale, and angle.
Figure~\ref{fig:rgb_thermal_misalignment} illustrates this misalignment.
This problem is documented in the DroneVehicle dataset, which provides
RGB-thermal pairs from a UAV perspective.
More than 20\% of bounding boxes in that dataset exhibit a position or
size offset of at least 3 pixels or an angular offset of at least
3 degrees.
Misalignment of this magnitude produces incorrect bounding box predictions
and undermines fusion methods that rely on aligned feature
maps~\cite{yuan2022crossModalAlignmentVehicleDetection,he2023misalignedRGBTadaptiveCalibration}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{images/rgb_thermal_misalignment_yuan_2022_crossmodal_alignment.png}
    \caption{
        The spatial modality misalignment problem.~\cite{yuan2022crossModalAlignmentVehicleDetection}
    }
    \label{fig:rgb_thermal_misalignment}
\end{figure}

However, this approach did not work because the WIRIS camera only
provides aligned images over the HDMI interface, which cannot be used
when distance measurements are required. Only the visible and thermal
streams are available there. All three streams are only accessible
simultaneously over Ethernet through separate RTSP streams, but in this
configuration, the visible and thermal cameras are not spatially aligned
or overlayed by the camera. Therefore, sensor alignment must be manually
implemented as described in section~\ref{chap:sensor_alignment}. On the
other hand, this separation offers more flexibility in developing the
object detection model, as the process is not constrained by the
cameras internal overlay method. By working with two complete, independent
video streams instead of a single overlayed stream, we gain access to
more information for detection.

\chapter{Dataset}
\label{chap:dataset}

To the best of our knowledge, no existing large-scale dataset fully satisfies the
requirements identified in for this the 3D-Net research project.
Specifically, no publicly available dataset combines a low-altitude side-view UAV
perspective with LWIR imagery and covers vehicle detection under adverse weather
and nighttime conditions.
For this reason, this project collects a new dataset for this project referred to as 3D-Net.
This chapter first describes the data collection process, then outlines existing
public UAV datasets and justifies the inclusion of public datasets for
this project.

\vspace{-2em}
\section{Data Collection}

As previously described in Chapter~\ref{chap:hardware}, the data collection is performed using the
Workswell WIRIS Enterprise multi-sensor system, which provides unaligned RGB and thermal
video streams. The collection process involves flying the drone equipped with the WIRIS camera
alongside road segments. Data was collected at the IG-Modellflugplatz Häusern in Ampass,
a model airfield in the Austrian Alps. The site features a road with regular traffic.
Unlike many locations in the Innsbruck area near the Innsbruck airport, it does not require
special permits or impose drone flight prohibitions. The data collection was conducted over
three separate sessions on different days. In total, eight scenes were recorded, each at a
different distance and viewing angle to the road. The drone was repositioned at a different
location along the airfield for each scene, which introduces additional variation in backgrounds
and vehicle appearances.
Samples of the recorded scenes are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:recorded_scenes}.

    \caption{
        Aligned sample frames from the recorded scenes. The top row shows RGB frames, while the bottom row shows the corresponding thermal frames.
    }
    \label{fig:recorded_scenes}
\end{figure}

The WIRIS camera was configured to capture optimal thermal data.
Material emissivity was set to 0.95, the standard value for rubber tires and
automotive paint as the primary materials of interest in vehicle
detection~\cite{hu2026VehicleTireTankTemperatureMonitoringThermalCameras}
as previously seen in \ref{fig:vehicle_thermal_radiation}.
The camera system automatically adjusts the temperature range to the minimum and maximum
of the current scene to maximize image contrast.
Since per-flight reconfiguration to environmental conditions is not foreseen,
ambient temperature and relative humidity were left at their default values of
20\,\textdegree C and 50\,\%.
The object distance was set to 30\,m to match the typical operating altitude of the drone.


\subsection{Sensor Alignment}
\label{chap:sensor_alignment}

The collected RGB and thermal video streams are spatially and temporally unaligned,
which requires correction before the data can be used for training or inference.
Existing RGB-thermal object detection methods~\cite{zhang2021GAFF,qingyun2021CFT,wan2025yolov11rgbt,li2019illuminationAwareFasterRCNN,zhang2019crossModalityInteractiveAttentionNetwork}
assume that input image pairs are at least weakly geometrically aligned.
Providing unaligned input degrades detection quality because spatially inconsistent
feature fusion produces imprecise bounding boxes, as illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:bounding_box_degradation_misalignment}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{images/misalignment_degrading_bounding_box_he_2023.png}
    \caption{
        Low-quality bounding boxes caused by spatial misalignment.
        Adapted from \cite{he2023misalignedRGBTadaptiveCalibration}.
    }
    \label{fig:bounding_box_degradation_misalignment}
\end{figure}

Physical sensor calibration through mechanical co-boresighting and manual per-frame
alignment by annotators are both labor-intensive approaches that are considered impractical for this
project~\cite{valverde2021selfSupervisedMultiObjectDetection, hwang2015multispectralPedestrianDetection}.
A more practical approach is to compute the geometric mapping between the two sensor views
and to match frames by timestamp.
This yields weakly aligned image pairs, which is consistent with the alignment quality
present in established RGB-thermal datasets~\cite{he2023misalignedRGBTadaptiveCalibration, yuan2022crossModalAlignmentVehicleDetection}.
The same transformations are applied at inference time to ensure that the model
receives consistently processed input.

Two distinct sources of misalignment were present in the recordings from the initial
data collection flight and are corrected in the following procedure.
Temporal misalignment arose from differing frame rates between the two cameras, and spatial
misalignment arose from physical differences in sensor placement and field of view.

\vspace{-0.6em}
\paragraph{Temporal Misalignment}
The temporal misalignment was primarily caused by differences in the sampling
rates of the two cameras. The RGB camera captured frames at 20\,fps, whereas
the thermal
camera operated at 21.2\,fps. As a result, a direct frame-by-frame overlay
was not possible, since the streams gradually drift apart over time. For
instance, the ratio $20 / 21.2 \approx 0.9434$ leads to a temporal drift of
approximately $3.4$\,seconds after one minute of recording.
Additionally, the recordings from both cameras did not start simultaneously,
introducing a constant temporal offset between the two streams.

To address these issues, a two-step synchronization process was applied.
First, the thermal video was temporally aligned to the RGB video by matching
frames based on their timestamps. For each RGB frame, the closest thermal
frame in time was identified using the presentation timestamps stored in the
frame metadata. These timestamps were converted from discrete clock ticks to
seconds by applying the corresponding timebase of each video stream. The
thermal frame with the smallest temporal difference to the RGB frame was then
selected, resulting in two videos with equal numbers of synchronized frames.

While this step eliminated the progressive temporal drift, a constant offset
between the videos remained due to different recording start times. This
offset was estimated by identifying a distinct and easily recognizable event
in both videos (e.g., two cars crossing). The difference between the
corresponding timestamps yielded an offset of 2.6\,seconds.
The final synchronization was achieved by shifting one video by this offset
and repeating the frame-matching procedure. This resulted in temporally
aligned video streams with a maximum frame pairing error of 55\,ms over
15-minute recordings.

The first problem that needed to be addressed for the sensor data
was temporal misalignment in the collected video samples during data
collection. This was due to the fact that the optical camera sampled
frames at a rate of 20 fps, while the thermal camera provides 21.2 fps.
This means that overlaying frame by frame was not possible as this
leads to significant temporal mismatch. For example, after 1 minute with
20/21.2 = 0.9434 which leads to a difference of 60s * 0.9434 = 3.4 seconds
after 1 minute already.
In addition, it was found that the videos collected during data collection did not start
at the same time, which means that the temporal misalignment is also because of this.

The first step to address this problem was to synchronize the videos by first
converting the thermal video to the same frame rate as the RGB video. This was done by
iterating through the RGB video frame by frame and for each RGB frame,
finding the closest thermal frame in time by using the presentation timestamp
included in the metadata of the video frames, multiplying this timestamp
by the corresponding timebase of the two videos to convert it from ticks, which are
integer time units used by media stream clocks, to seconds, and then finding the
thermal frame with the closest second timestamp to the RGB frame timestamp and matching
them together, creating two videos with the same number of synchronized frames.

After this process, the videos were no longer drifting apart temporally over time, yet
there was still a constant temporal offset between the two videos due to the differing
start times. This offset was estimated by selecting a well suited and easily identifiable
event in both videos, such as two cars crossing each other.
From this event, the timestamp
of the event in both videos was found and the difference between them was calculated
to obtain the temporal offset of 2.6 seconds. After this step, the videos were synchronized by shifting
one of the videos by this temporal offset and applying the same frame matching algorithm
as before with the new offset. This resulted in two videos that were temporally aligned
with a maximum temporal frame pairing offset of 55 ms across 15 minute videos.

\vspace{-0.6em}
\paragraph{Spatial Misalignment}
After temporal alignment, spatial alignment between the two modalities was
addressed. The spatial misalignment originates from three independent
factors, which are the physical separation of the sensors causing parallax,
the differing fields of view (FOV) of the cameras, and a misalignment in
the optical axes of the two cameras called boresight error.
The camera parameters used to compute the required corrections are
listed in Table~\ref{tab:camera_params}.

\paragraph{FOV error}

The thermal camera covers a smaller angular region of the scene
due to its narrower FOV (Table~\ref{tab:camera_params}).
To quantify this, separate horizontal and vertical scale factors
$s_H$ and $s_V$ are defined as the ratio of the real-world
extents $H$ captured by each camera at equal depth $Z$.
The pinhole model is used to derive this ratio. It approximates
the camera projection by treating the lens as a single point,
thereby neglecting distortion at the edges of the image, as
illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:pinhole_fov_scale}. Under this
model, the half-extent at depth $Z$ is $Z\tan(\mathrm{FOV}/2)$.
Since both cameras observe the same depth $Z$, it cancels in
the ratio and both scale factors reduce to the depth-independent
tangent ratio of the FOVs:

\begin{align}
    s_H & = \frac{H_{thm}^H}
    {H_{vis}^H}
    = \frac{Z\tan(\mathrm{FOV_{thm}^H}/2)}
    {Z\tan(\mathrm{FOV_{vis}^H}/2)}
    = \frac{\tan(\mathrm{FOV_{thm}^H}/2)}
    {\tan(\mathrm{FOV_{vis}^H}/2)}
    = \frac{\tan(22.5^\circ)}{\tan(31.85^\circ)}
    = 0.667                  \\[6pt]
    s_V & = \frac{H_{thm}^V}
    {H_{vis}^V}
    = \frac{Z\tan(\mathrm{FOV_{thm}^V}/2)}
    {Z\tan(\mathrm{FOV_{vis}^V}/2)}
    = \frac{\tan(\mathrm{FOV_{thm}^V}/2)}
    {\tan(\mathrm{FOV_{vis}^V}/2)}
    = \frac{\tan(18.5^\circ)}{\tan(19.30^\circ)}
    = 0.955
\end{align}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/pinhole_fov_scale.png}
    \caption{
        The pinhole model for FOV scaling in one dimension.
    }
    \label{fig:pinhole_fov_scale}
\end{figure}

The thermal image therefore covers 66.7\,\% of the visible frame
horizontally and 95.6\,\% vertically. Multiplying these fractions
by the visible resolution gives the target size of the thermal
content on the visible canvas: $1280 \times 0.667 = 853.5$\,px
wide and $720 \times 0.955 = 688.0$\,px tall. Stretching the
thermal frame to this target size yields pixel-grid scale factors
of $\mathrm{scale}_x = 853.5/640 = 1.334$ and
$\mathrm{scale}_y = 688.0/512 = 1.344$. Assuming a shared
optical axis, the scaled thermal region is then centred on the
visible canvas, giving translations
$t_x = (1280 - 853.5)/2 = 213.3$\,px and
$t_y = (720 - 688.0)/2 = 16.0$\,px. These four values are
combined into a single affine transformation, which is
applied to the thermal image to align it to the visible frame:

\begin{equation}
    M =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        1.3335 & 0      & 213.3 \\
        0      & 1.3436 & 16.0
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Boresight Error}

After FOV-based alignment, a residual constant pixel offset persists
across all scene depths. This indicates a boresight error, an angular
misalignment between the optical axes of the two cameras.
Such misalignment introduces a constant angular offset in the observed
scene, which manifests as a constant pixel offset in the images regardless
of object depth. The offset is depth-independent because each pixel subtends
a fixed solid angle. As distance increases, the physical separation between
pixel rays grows at the same rate as the inter-camera displacement of an
object caused by boresight offset. Therefore, the displacement measured
in pixels remains constant. Consequently, a boresight error is equivalent
to a uniform shift in pixel space.

The pixel offsets were empirically measured as $\Delta x = -30$\,px and
$\Delta y = -10$\,px. These are added directly to the centering
translation, yielding the corrected affine transformation:

\begin{equation}
    M =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        1.3335 & 0      & 183.3 \\
        0      & 1.3436 & 6.0
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}

From the empirical pixel offsets, the corresponding angular misalignment can be estimated using the
pinhole camera model. The focal length in pixels is derived from the FOV and resolution as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:boresight_misalignment_approximation}a:
%
\begin{equation}
    f_{\mathrm{px}} = \frac{\text{resolution}/2}{\tan(\text{FOV}/2)}
\end{equation}
%
Evaluating for each axis gives $f_{\mathrm{vis},H} = 1030.2$\,px and $f_{\mathrm{vis},V} = 1028.6$\,px.
The angular offsets then follow from the pixel offset and focal length as illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:boresight_misalignment_approximation}b, which can lie well
within typical manufacturing tolerances for camera alignment:

\begin{equation}
    \theta_H = \arctan\!\left(\tfrac{30}{1030.2}\right) = 1.67^\circ, \qquad
    \theta_V = \arctan\!\left(\tfrac{10}{1028.6}\right) = 0.56^\circ
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/boresight_misalignment_approximation.png}
    \caption{
        The pinhole model for boresight angle approximation.
        (a) Focal length in pixels derived from FOV and resolution.
        (b) Angular misalignment derived from pixel offset and focal length.
    }
    \label{fig:boresight_misalignment_approximation}
\end{figure}


\paragraph{Parallax Error}

Even after applying the corrected affine transformation, a residual
misalignment remains, especially for objects that are close to the
camera. This effect is known as parallax. Parallax occurs
because the two camera lenses are physically separated by a small
distance, the baseline $b$. As a result, each lens observes the same
scene from a slightly different viewpoint. While distant objects
appear almost identical in both views, nearby objects shift noticeably
between them due to this change in perspective.
The resulting pixel shift depends on both the baseline $b$ and the
distance to the observed object $d$. As illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:parallax_error}, the shift can be approximated as:

\begin{equation}
    \Delta\mathrm{px} = \tan(\theta) \times f_{\mathrm{vis,px}}
    = \frac{b}{d} \times f_{\mathrm{vis,px}}
    \label{eq:parallax_shift}
\end{equation}.
\cite[pp.~701]{torralba2024foundationsComputerVision}

This relationship shows that the pixel shift decreases with
increasing distance $d$, and approaches zero as $d \to \infty$.
Consequently, a constant boresight correction provides a good
approximation for distant scenes, but becomes increasingly
inaccurate for nearby objects. For the drone application considered
here, the camera baseline is $b = 0.10$,m and the operating distance
is about $d = 30$,m. Substituting these values into
Equation~\ref{eq:parallax_shift} yields a maximum parallax shift
of $3.43~\text{px}$.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{images/parallax_shift.png}
    \caption{
        The pinhole camera model used to approximate
        the parallax-induced pixel shift $\Delta\mathrm{px}$.
    }
    \label{fig:parallax_error}
\end{figure}

Although the maximum parallax error this is relatively small, it
is not negligible. Accurately correcting parallax across
a scene would require depth information for each pixel, which can
only be obtained using a depth sensor or stereo reconstruction.
Such corrections cannot be achieved with a single global affine
transformation. For this reason, the residual error is considered
acceptable for the current application and is ignored. However, it
should be taken into account if the drone operates at significantly
shorter distances in the future.

Figure~\ref{fig:camera_alignment_result} shows qualitative results of the
final RGB--thermal alignment. The alignment is not perfect but
provides a good approximation across the scene. In particular,
Figure~\ref{fig:camera_alignment_result}(a) illustrates the parallax effect
for nearby objects. In contrast,
distant objects, such as the vehicles in (b), are well aligned.
Minor residual errors can still be observed as shown in (c), which are
mainly attributed to drone oscillations combined with slight differences in
frame capture times, as well as differing camera-dependent distortions
near the image edges visible for the vehicle near the top.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/camera_alignment_results.png}
    \caption{
        Qualitative RGB--thermal alignment results (thermal in BWRGB).
        (a) Near-object parallax on a bench.
        (b) Accurate vehicle alignment.
        (c) Occasional residual misalignment after correction.
    }
    \label{fig:camera_alignment_result}
\end{figure}

The alignment procedure described above was first developed and applied in
postprocessing to the recordings from the initial data collection flight.
Both the spatial transformation matrix and the temporal synchronization approach
were subsequently integrated into the object tracking service described in
Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}, which runs on a Raspberry Pi mounted on the drone.
A dedicated recording mode was added to the service that applies the spatial
transformation to each incoming thermal frame in real time.
For temporal alignment, the service maintains rolling buffers of 30 frames per
modality and selects the temporally closest thermal frame for each incoming
visible-spectrum frame by comparing presentation timestamps.
This integration ensured that all subsequent data collection flights produced
spatially and temporally aligned recordings without postprocessing.
As a result, the training data and the model input during production are
processed by the same system.
This guarantees consistent input representations across data collection and deployment.

\vspace{-1em}
\subsection{Annotation}

The aligned RGB and thermal video streams were sampled to extract individual frames for annotation.
Every third frame was selected from each video.
Sampling at this rate reduces the redundancy inherent in high-frame-rate recordings, where consecutive
frames differ very little in content.
At a camera rate of 20\,fps, every third frame corresponds to an inter-sample interval of 0.15\,s.
At a typical road speed of 70\,km/h ($\approx 19.4$\,m/s), a vehicle at the nominal operating
distance of 30\,m travels 2.92\,m during this interval.
Assuming motion perpendicular to the camera's optical axis and using similar triangles with the
focal length derived in Section~\ref{chap:sensor_alignment} ($f_\mathrm{px} \approx 1030$\,px),
this corresponds to a pixel shift of
%
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\mathrm{px} = \frac{2.92}{30} \times 1030 \approx 100\,\mathrm{px},
\end{equation}
%
which is sufficient to ensure that adjacent samples show the vehicle in
different positions, thereby avoiding dataset redundancy.
In practice, this value represents an upper bound, as the drone was also oriented with a forward
component relative to the road. This reduced the lateral displacement
and consequently increased the number of consecutive samples in which vehicles remain within the field of view.

Annotations were produced using a combination of the FiftyOne dataset management
library~\cite{moore2020fiftyone} and the Computer Vision Annotation Tool
(CVAT)~\cite{cvat2019} as the annotation backend.
FiftyOne organises samples in a queryable format and allows arbitrary metadata fields such as
recording session, date, and dataset split to be attached to each sample, making it straightforward
to filter, modify, and access the dataset both programmatically and via a web interface.
CVAT provides a web-based labeling interface that enables annotators to draw detection bounding boxes
quickly over image sequences using keyboard shortcuts.
FiftyOne was used to prepare and push annotation tasks to CVAT and to import the completed labels back
into the dataset.
The raw images were stored in an S3-compatible object storage bucket hosted on the Plusserver cloud.
Figure~\ref{fig:annotation_tools} shows the interfaces of both tools.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/cvat_fiftyone_showcase.png}
    \caption{
        CVAT annotation interface with a car bounding box (left) and FiftyOne dataset browser
        displaying samples with metadata including location, date, and split (right).
    }
    \label{fig:annotation_tools}
\end{figure}

The six annotation classes are car, truck, bus, motorcycle, bicycle, and person, chosen to align
with the public datasets used for training as described in Section~\ref{sec:public_datasets}.
To reduce inter-annotator ambiguity, vehicles were classified using the Bernard Gruppe 12+1
taxonomy~\cite{bernardGruppe12plus1Classification}, which extends the BASt 8+1
system~\cite{bastVehicleClassification} and is already familiar to all annotators in the team.
This internal taxonomy was then mapped to the six dataset classes as specified in full in
Appendix~\ref{app:labeling_guidelines}.

The annotation was performed by a team of three annotators, including the author, each with at least
one year of prior annotation experience at Bernard Gruppe.
Samples were organised into tasks of 200 frames, and annotators were instructed to take a break after
completing each task to prevent fatigue and maintain consistency.
Before work began, a labeling guideline was prepared and distributed to the full team, it is reproduced
in Appendix~\ref{app:labeling_guidelines}.
The combined annotation effort amounted to approximately \{N\_hours\} hours.
After the initial annotation pass, the author reviewed all samples to identify and correct
inconsistencies.

The most consequential rules from the labeling guidelines are as follows.
A vehicle is annotated only when it occupies at least $16 \times 12$ pixels, at least 30\,\% of it is
visible within the frame, and its class can be determined from the current frame alone without
consulting neighbouring frames.
Bounding boxes must be axis-aligned with the image, enclose only the visible portion of a vehicle, and
stop at the image boundary rather than estimating the occluded extent.
In low-light frames where the RGB channel is ambiguous, annotators were instructed to consult the
co-registered thermal image, as vehicles and people are thermally distinct from their surroundings
even at night.

Only the RGB frames were annotated in CVAT, with the same bounding boxes and class labels then applied
to the spatially aligned thermal frames.
Adding the thermal images as contextual side-by-side views within CVAT was considered to assist
annotators in low-light frames, but FiftyOne does not yet support this CVAT feature at the time of
writing. % FiftyOne Feature Request: https://github.com/voxel51/fiftyone/issues/3673
As an alternative, annotators were instructed to access the corresponding thermal image via the S3 URL
when the visible channel was ambiguous.
A limitation of this approach is that the residual spatial misalignment discussed in
Section~\ref{chap:sensor_alignment} may cause bounding boxes to fit the thermal channel less precisely.

\subsection{Thermal Color Format Conversion}

During the first data collection flight session, the camera was set to BWRGB mode, which encodes
the recordings in a non-standard format where the thermal data moves as thermal intensity increases
from black to white to blue to green to red and then pink as the hottest pixels. This format is not ideal
as most other RGB-thermal datasets use a standard grayscale format for the thermal channel. Therefore,
the thermal data of the first flight session was postprocessed to convert it from BWRGB to grayscale.
This was done using the palette file provided by the Workswell Support for the WIRIS camera, which maps 256 BWRGB
colors to their corresponding thermal intensity values.

The data postprocessing script reads the BWRGB thermal images, applies the palette mapping to convert each
pixel's BWRGB value to its corresponding grayscale intensity, and saves the resulting grayscale thermal
images. If a color value in the BWRGB image does not have a corresponding entry in the palette, it is
assigned a default intensity value of 0. This occurence was observed for a big number of pixels in the thermal
images, which is expected to be due to video format compression, which introduces slight variations in
pixel values that may not exactly match the palette entries. Therefore, a nearest neighbour interpolation
was applied to assign the closest palette intensity value to each pixel.
This process significatntly improved the quality of the postprocessed thermal images with
only a small number of pixels black noise. This noise happens for areas that quickly moves from blue to white
in the preprocessed images, as the transition in the compressed video format results in quite dark areas that are most similar to way darker areas
in the original BWRGB palette. This can be seen in the color palette to the right of the Figure~\ref{fig:thermal_postprocessing_comparison}
where the mentioned pixels are moved from the transition between blue and white down to the transition between black and white. An example of the resulting postprocessed thermal images
for the original, simply mapped and interpolated versions are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:thermal_postprocessing_comparison}.

\subsection{Dataset Statistics}

After annotation, the self-collected dataset comprises $12263$ pixel-aligned image pairs of dimension
$852 \times 672$ containing a total of $9060$ bounding boxes across six classes.
Cars ($49.7\%$) and trucks ($31.7\%$) together constitute $81.4\%$ of all labels.
The remaining $18.6\%$ is distributed across persons ($15.0\%$), bicycles ($2.3\%$), buses ($1.1\%$),
and motorcycles ($0.2\%$).
The dataset was split by assigning the first $70\,\%$ of frames from each recorded scene to the training set,
the next $15\,\%$ to validation, and the final $15\,\%$ to the test set.
To ensure that the underrepresented bus, bicycle, and motorcycle classes appear in all splits,
their instances were explicitly distributed across the three partitions in the same manner.
Figure~\ref{fig:self_collected_stats} (left) shows the class distribution across the train, validation, and test splits.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/3d_net_dataset_statistics_vertical.png}

        and normalized bounding box width-height scatter plot (bottom) are shown.

All classes occupy approximately $0.1\%$ to $1\%$ of the normalized image area, which is in accordance with
other UAV vehicle object detection datasets~\cite{feng2023rtdodDataset,sun2022droneVehicleRGBThermalUAVDataset}.
Truck bounding boxes form a narrow peak in the area distribution.
This concentration reflects a single stationary truck that was present in the background of 2 scenes during the collection flights.
Generally, the classes show a peak around $0.1\%$ with a long tail towards larger sizes as the vehicles move closer
to the camera in the recording scenes that are angled with a forward component relative to the road.

The shape scatter reveals the relationship between object height and width.
Persons appear as tall, narrow boxes because their vertical body silhouette dominates from an aerial viewpoint.
Bicycles share a similar aspect ratio but shift toward slightly greater width.
Cars and buses form a compact diagonal band with low aspect-ratio variance.
Trucks occupy the largest bounding box sizes and show the widest spread in both width and height, but the distribution
goes towards wide horizontal bounding boxes with increasing size.



All recordings were captured during daytime because operational restrictions at Bernard Gruppe
prohibited night flights.
No adverse weather conditions occurred during the collection flights, so the dataset does not cover
rain or fog scenarios.
The first collection flight took place in February 2026 and therefore includes snow conditions.
In addition, motorcycle and bus instances are underrepresented, with at most $12$ and $86$
samples in the training split respectively.
These limitations motivate the inclusion of the public datasets described in Section~\ref{sec:public_datasets}.

\vspace{-2em}
\section{Public Visible-Thermal Datasets}
\label{sec:public_datasets}
\vspace{-0.5em}

UAV datasets typically distinguish between nadir views, where the camera points
straight downward, and oblique views, where the camera is tilted at an angle to the ground.
Among UAV-based RGB-thermal datasets, the DroneVehicle dataset
\cite{sun2022droneVehicleRGBThermalUAVDataset} is one of the largest,
comprising 28,439 aligned RGB and thermal image pairs with 953,087
oriented bounding boxes across five vehicle classes including car,
truck, bus, van, and freight car. The data were captured from UAVs
flying at altitudes between 80 and 120 meters with oblique viewing angles
from 15 to 45 degrees, and include daytime, nighttime, and
dark-night conditions. Similarly, the RTDOD dataset
\cite{feng2023rtdodDataset} provides 16,192 aligned RGB-thermal
image pairs. It includes ten object classes such as car,
truck, bus, motorcycle, and person, and covers diverse environments
including suburbs, highways, and bridges under sunny, rainy, foggy,
and nighttime scenarios with varying altitudes and nadir to oblique viewing angles. The VTUAV dataset
\cite{zhang2022VTUAVDataset} contains around 500 RGB-thermal
sequences totaling about 1.7 million frame pairs captured at
low to moderate altitudes between 5 and 20 meters with nadir and
oblique views, primarily designed for visible-thermal object tracking
rather than static detection. Its detection-focused variant, VTUAV-det
\cite{zhang2023RGBTTinyPersonDetectionVTUAVDetDataset}, includes only
the person class. The M3OT dataset \cite{nie2025m3ot} provides 21,580
aligned RGB-thermal UAV image pairs captured from high altitudes
between 100 and 200 meters during day and night, focusing on small
object detection and containing only a single vehicle class.
TrafficNight is an aligned RGB, thermal, and depth dataset comprising
2,200 annotated samples captured from high altitude nadir perspective, spanning six
vehicle classes with a particular emphasis on trucks and semi-trailers
in low-light and nighttime traffic scenarios.
\cite{zhang2024trafficNightDataset}

Other UAV datasets provide only one of the two modalities. Thermal-only
datasets include HIT-UAV \cite{suo2023hitUAVDataset}, which contains 2,898
thermal images captured at altitudes between 60 and 130 meters with oblique to nadir viewing
angles from 30 to 90 degrees, covering day and night scenarios but including
only one true vehicle class. The MONET thermal dataset
\cite{riz2023monetDataset} expands UAV-based thermal data with approximately
53,000 thermal frames captured at altitudes between 20 and 130 meters with
oblique forward-looking views. RGB data was also collected but not released
due to privacy concerns. MONET contains day and night recordings and provides
rich drone metadata such as GPS, altitude, and gimbal orientation, but
includes only two primary object classes, person and vehicle.

In addition to thermal-only datasets, several UAV datasets provide only RGB
imagery. The UAVDT dataset \cite{du2018UAVDTDataset} contains approximately
80,000 RGB images captured at altitudes from 10 to above 70 meters, including
front, side, and bird's-eye views with three vehicle classes under day, night,
and adverse weather conditions. The AU-AIR dataset \cite{bozcan2020auAirDataset}
provides 32,823 RGB frames from low altitudes between 5 and 30 meters with
viewing angles from 45 to 90 degrees and eight vehicle classes but only daytime
conditions. The VAID dataset \cite{li2020aerialVehicleDetectionVAIDDataset}
includes 5,937 RGB aerial images with seven vehicle classes captured at
approximately 90 meters from a nadir perspective, without nighttime or
adverse weather data. The VEDAI dataset \cite{razakarivony2016VEDAIdataset}
contains 1,210 aerial RGB and infrared images from a nadir perspective
with six vehicle categories including car, pick-up, truck, camping car,
tractor, and van, but does not focus on nighttime or adverse weather conditions.

Vehicle-mounted multimodal RGB-thermal datasets include the FLIR ADAS v2
dataset \cite{teledyne_2022_flirAdasV2Dataset} with 26,442 frames and more
than 520,000 bounding boxes across nine vehicle and person classes under day,
night, fog, and adverse weather conditions, but only the test set is temporally
and spatially aligned and the perspective is forward-looking. The KAIST ADAS
dataset \cite{choi2018kaist} provides about 95,000 aligned RGB-thermal pairs
captured with a beam-splitter camera, including day and night, but mainly
focuses on pedestrians with only one vehicle class. The M3FD dataset
\cite{liu2022m3fdDataset} contains 4,200 aligned RGB-thermal pairs captured
with a synchronized binocular system in daytime, overcast, nighttime, and
challenging conditions, including multiple vehicle classes but remaining
relatively small. The MSRS dataset \cite{tang2022piafusionMSRSDataset} provides
1,444 aligned RGB-thermal pairs from road scenes in day and night but includes
only one vehicle class. The Infraparis dataset
\cite{franchi2024infraparisRGBDepthThermalDataset} expands multimodal sensing
with 12,084 RGB, depth, and thermal image pairs with segmentation masks and
bounding boxes, including several vehicle classes but not explicitly focusing
on adverse weather. The Multispectral dataset
\cite{takumi2017multispectralDatasetAutonomousVehicles} contains 7,512 aligned
RGB, thermal, near-infrared, and mid-infrared image pairs under day and night
but includes only includes person, bike, and vehicle object classes. The RASMD
dataset \cite{jin2026rasmd} provides approximately 100,000 RGB-SWIR image
pairs under adverse weather with multiple vehicle classes but does not include
thermal LWIR imagery.

Other RGB-only vehicle-perspective datasets focus on adverse weather.
The DAWN dataset \cite{kenk2020dawn} provides 1,000 RGB images for vehicle
detection covering five classes: car, truck, bus, person, and a combined motorcycle
and bicycle class, with a total of 7,845 bounding boxes. DAWN includes adverse
lighting and weather, but does not cover nighttime. The ACDC dataset
\cite{sakaridis2025acdcDatasetAdverseConditions} contains 5,509 RGB images for
semantic segmentation with the same relevant classes and pixel-level annotations.
From this set, 4,006 images are taken in adverse conditions including nighttime.
Both datasets lack thermal modality. Finally, the LLVIP dataset \cite{jia2021llvip}
provides 16,836 aligned RGB-thermal pairs from fixed oblique surveillance cameras
for pedestrian detection in low-light conditions. Table \ref{tab:rgb_thermal_datasets}
summarizes the characteristics of all discussed datasets.

\section{Dataset Composition}

Overall, numerous datasets exist across UAV-based and
vehicle-mounted perspectives. Yet, each dataset lacks at least one
component for the targeted application, such as low-altitude
side-view UAV perspective, multiple vehicle classes, aligned
RGB-thermal data, and coverage of adverse weather and nighttime
conditions.
For this reason, the dataset for this project is constructed
by combining existing datasets that contain the most relevant data
and supplementing them with additional data collection specifically
using our drone setup.

In this work, a data pool consisting of multiple datasets is designed to build a gen-
eral person and vehicle detector in the combined visible and thermal-infrared
spectrum.
The chosen datasets for training include the RTDOD dataset, which
provides a modest number of aligned RGB-thermal pairs from varying
altitudes in a side-view UAV perspective with multiple vehicle classes
and person annotations in day and night under diverse weather conditions,
The DroneVehicle dataset,
which provides a larger number of aligned RGB-thermal pairs from a
high altitude UAV, partially from  side-view
perspective with multiple vehicle classes in day and night, and the
M3FD dataset, which offers aligned RGB-thermal data from a
vehicle-top-mounted perspective with several vehicle classes in diverse
weather conditions to increase the amount of close side-view as well
as front- and back-facing vehicle data
more similar to the 20m flight height in our scenario than the high altitude
perspective present in DroneVehicle and partially in RTDOD.
This aims at building a more general model that can perform well across different
scenarios and perspectives as shown in \cite{becker2025fusionOrConfusionDatasetPooling},
though the performance in each specific domain was not significantly increased
by increasing data diversity and volume through data pooling, but matched
across all domains on a single detector.

The DroneVehicle
dataset could not be downloaded from the official Baidu Yun dataset.
This is due to the fact that it is not possible to create Baidu Yun
accounts using austrian phone numbers, and the dataset is not available
for download without an account.
A DroneVehicle dataset from Huggingface needed to be downloaded, which
contained ~20 corrupt samples.
Similarly, the RTDOD dataset could not be downloaded from the official
Baidu Yun dataset, and the secondary source published by the authors
on Google Drive and Huggingface contained only a small subset of
the original dataset. For this reason, the paid platform Baidu
Erranium was used to download the full RTDOD dataset.

The visible-infrared datasets differ in their annotation formats and class definitions.
To standardize labels across datasets, we map sub-categories to unified super-categories
as shown in Table~\ref{tab:dataset_class_merge}, following~\cite{becker2025fusionOrConfusionDatasetPooling}.
For DroneVehicle, the van class is merged into car, as vans are visually similar to cars,
a grouping consistent with community derivations of the dataset
for YOLO~\cite{redzapdos123_droneVehicleYOLOv11OBB} that collapse both into a
single small-vehicle category.
Similarly, the freight car class is merged into truck, as both designate large
cargo-carrying vehicles.
Motorcycle and person classes, present only in RTDOD and M3FD, are included without modification
and non-vehicle classes are discarded.
The OBB annotations of DroneVehicle, defined by four corner points, are converted to
horizontal bounding boxes (HBB) by computing the minimum enclosing axis-aligned rectangle,
matching the HBB format of RTDOD and M3FD.

The dataset splits were created by using the individual splits
provided by each dataset, where possible. The DroneVehicle dataset
provides official train, validation and test splits, which were used
directly.
The M3FD dataset does not provide unified data splits.
The images in this dataset are sampled from video sequences,
which means adjacent frames may contain identical content.
In this context, a random splitting approach results in information leakage
between the train and validation sets. Therefore, we utilize the
M3FD-zxSplit proposed by \cite{zhang2024rethinkingEarlyFusion},
which splits the dataset based on video segments to avoid information leakage.
The first 70\% of images in each video segment are collected as the
train set and the remaining 30\% of images are used as the validation set.
In this work, the validation set is further split into validation and test
sets, where the first 50\% of the validation set is used for validation
and the remaining 50\% is used for testing so that the test set is
further apart from the train set.
The RTDOD dataset provides official trainval and test splits.
The trainval split was split in the same way as the M3FD dataset,
where the last 10\% of the images in each video segment belonging
to the trainval set were used as
the validation set, and the rest were used for training.

\subsection{Thermal Preprocessing}
\vspace{-0.2em}

Infrared imagery is sensitive to variations in temperature,
sensor characteristics, and environmental conditions. Thus,
preprocessing is a crucial step for reducing domain gaps between datasets.
However, most publicly available infrared datasets only provide limited
thermal range  processed images with dynamic temperature scales rather
than full radiometric data, which
hinders the effectiveness of unified preprocessing across a dataset pool.

In \cite{becker2025fusionOrConfusionDatasetPooling}, the three preprocessing
techniques were evaluated to investigate their
impact on dataset alignment.
Histogram equalization is a commonly used image enhancement technique
that redistributes pixel intensities to
achieve a more uniform histogram and thereby increase global contrast by
stretching the intensity range \cite{herrmann2018cnnInfraredPersonDetectionDomainAdaptation}.
Contrast limited adaptive histogram equalization
(CLAHE) \cite{pizer1987adaptiveHistogramEqualization} improves local contrast by dividing images into contextual regions
and applying adaptive enhancement within each region.
FieldScale, specifically designed for thermal infrared imagery,
applies locality-aware adaptive rescaling based on spatially varying
intensity fields rather than global or strictly local transformations \cite{gil2024fieldscale}.
Figure~\ref{fig:thermal_preprocessing} shows an example of HE, CLAHE,
and FieldScale applied to an image from the FLIR(v2) dataset \cite{teledyne_2022_flirAdasV2Dataset}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/thermal_preprocessing_becker_2025_fusion_confusion.png}
    \caption{
        The results of preprocessing
        methods HE, CLAHE, and Fieldscale.
        \cite{becker2025fusionOrConfusionDatasetPooling}
    }
    \label{fig:thermal_preprocessing}
\end{figure}

However, the experiments in \citet{becker2025fusionOrConfusionDatasetPooling}
showed that applying these preprocessing techniques to already low dynamic
range infrared images did not lead to significant performance improvements
across datasets, and trained models appeared to internally compensate for
contrast differences. This was further supported by dimensionality reduction
exploration, where the average pairwise distance between dataset
clusters remained unchanged after preprocessing.
Consequently,
unified thermal preprocessing
is not performed for the dataset pool.

The three datasets were uploaded to an Amazon S3-compatible bucket
hosted on the PlusServer cloud platform, which serves as the centralized
remote storage backend for FiftyOne, an open-source dataset curation and
visualization tool that organizes machine learning datasets by storing
sample-level annotations, metadata, and file references (URLs) in a
structured, queryable format. Maintaining the datasets in a
cloud-accessible location is important for the remote training pipeline
managed by ClearML as further outlined in Section~\ref{sec:model_training_pipeline}.

an open-source machine learning operations (MLOps)
platform responsible for orchestrating and monitoring machine learning
experiments. During training runs, ClearML dispatches workloads to the
GPU workers hosted on Bernard's GPU Rack. The workers must retrieve the
required dataset assets at runtime from the S3 bucket through FiftyOne.

\chapter{Object Detection}
\label{chap:object_detection}

Object detection is the task of localizing and classifying all objects present in an image.
Each detected object is represented as a tuple of a bounding box, parameterized as center
coordinates, width, and height, and a class label~\cite{zou2023objectDetectionSurvey, redmon2016YOLO}.
Detection models are grouped into three families, namely proposal-based detectors,
grid-based detectors, and query-based detectors~\cite{bhowmik2023unionOverIntersections}.
These families are briefly introduced and justification for the choice of grid-based detectors is given.

\vspace{-2em}
\section{Proposal-based Detectors}
\vspace{-0.5em}

Proposal-based detectors operate in two sequential stages.
A region proposal method first generates a set of candidate regions likely to contain objects.
Each candidate region then undergoes independent classification and bounding box refinement.
One of the earliest methods, R-CNN~\cite{girshick2014RCNN}, generates region proposals without machine learning using the
selective search algorithm~\cite{uijlings2013selectiveSearch} and then applies a CNN classifier to each cropped proposal independently.
Fast R-CNN~\cite{girshick2015fastRCNN} improves efficiency by running a single CNN pass over the full image and
extracting per-region features through ROI pooling, which converts the variable-size proposal regions into a fixed-size representation.
This avoids redundant per-crop CNN evaluations and substantially reduces inference time.
Fast R-CNN also introduces anchor boxes, which are predefined bounding box templates of various sizes and aspect ratios
used as reference points for object localization~\cite{girshick2015fastRCNN}.
Faster R-CNN~\cite{ren2016fasterRCNN} replaced selective search with a learned Region Proposal Network (RPN).
This enables end-to-end training of the complete two-stage pipeline.
The Feature Pyramid Network (FPN)~\cite{lin2017featurePyramidNetworks} further extended these architectures by building
a multi-scale feature hierarchy from a single input image.
Before FPN, most detectors operated only on the feature maps of the network's final layer.
Although deep features are beneficial for category recognition, they lack the spatial resolution needed for accurate localization.
FPN addresses this by enabling detection at multiple network depths simultaneously, which improves detection of small objects in particular.
These fundamental proposal-based architectures are illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:proposal_based_architecture}.
Proposal-based detectors achieve slightly higher detection accuracy and better object localization than single-stage detectors,
but at inference times two to three times higher~\cite{carranza2020performanceSingleTwoStageDetectors, zou2023objectDetectionSurvey, advzemovic2025deepLearningMultiObjectTrackingSurvey, li2024developmentObjectDetectionSurvey}.

\vspace{-2em}
\section{Grid-based Detectors}
\vspace{-0.5em}

Modern single-stage detectors share a common three-component architecture, illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:grid_based_architecture}.
The backbone is a deep convolutional network that processes the input image and produces multi-scale feature maps.
The neck aggregates and refines these feature maps across scales to improve detection of objects at different sizes.
The head consumes the neck output and directly predicts bounding box coordinates and class probabilities for each candidate location~\cite{terven2024yoloReview}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{images/yolov11n_architecture_wang_2025.png}
    \caption{
        The YOLOv11n architecture, including the backbone, neck, and detection head \cite{wang2025raCottnet}.
    }
    \label{fig:grid_based_architecture}
\end{figure}

YOLO (You Only Look Once)~\cite{redmon2016YOLO} is the dominant family of grid-based detectors.
It processes the full image in a single forward pass.
The image is partitioned into a regular grid in which each cell predicts bounding box coordinates and class labels directly.
The family has evolved through several generations.
Each generation refined the backbone, neck, or head design~\cite{kotthapalli2025yolov1Toyolov11}.

The original YOLOv1 framed detection as a regression problem and achieved real-time speeds, but produced imprecise localizations and struggled with small objects.
YOLOv2~\cite{redmon2017yolo9000} introduced anchor boxes, which serve as predefined bounding box templates, together with batch normalization and multi-scale training.
YOLOv3~\cite{redmon2018yolov3} adopted FPN-style multi-scale prediction in the neck, which improved small-object recall.
YOLOv4~\cite{bochkovskiy2020yolov4} established the modern backbone/neck/head template by pairing a CSPDarkNet~\cite{wang2020cspnet} backbone with a PANet~\cite{liu2018PANet} neck.
PANet extends the standard FPN top-down upsampling with a subsequent bottom-up path that shortens the information route from low-level spatial features to higher-level predictions.
This preserves precise localization signals from early layers, which improves object localization accuracy.
The neck in Figure~\ref{fig:grid_based_architecture} illustrates this pattern, where FPN upsampling is followed by a downsampling stage~\cite{liu2018PANet}.
YOLOv5~\cite{khanam2024yolov5} ported this architecture to PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019pytorch}, which greatly broadened accessibility.
Later versions refined the head design.
YOLOv6~\cite{li2022yolov6} introduced decoupled classification and localization branches, and YOLOv8~\cite{yaseen2024yolov8} adopted an anchor-free design.
YOLOv10~\cite{wang2024yolov10} and YOLOv11~\cite{khanam2024yolov11} focused on efficiency through improved backbone and neck modules.
Both models achieve competitive accuracy with substantially fewer parameters than preceding versions~\cite{advzemovic2025deepLearningMultiObjectTrackingSurvey}.
In this work, YOLOv11n is used, as it achieved the best performance on the COCO dataset after compilation to the Hailo-8 NPU, as provided by the
Hailo Model Explorer~\cite{hailoModelExplorer2026}.

The performance of both proposal-based and grid-based detectors during inference depends on
Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS) to remove duplicate detections and retain the most confident
predictions~\cite{girshick2014RCNN}. The detector first produces a large number of raw bounding
boxes that may contain duplicates at slightly different locations. NMS then retains the prediction
with the highest confidence score and suppresses the overlapping candidates. The Intersection over
Union (IoU) overlap threshold that controls this suppression requires manual tuning to avoid
discarding valid detections as
duplicates~\cite{advzemovic2025deepLearningMultiObjectTrackingSurvey, zhao2024DETRsBeatYolosRealTimeObjectDetection}.
Most YOLO variants rely on NMS during inference, although end-to-end training approaches such as
those in YOLOv10 reduce this dependency~\cite{ge2021yolox}.

\vspace{-2em}
\section{Query-based Detectors}
\vspace{-0.5em}

DETR (DEtection TRansformer)~\cite{carion2020DETR} reformulates object detection as a set prediction problem.
The model consists of four main components, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:detr_architecture}.
A CNN backbone first extracts image features. A transformer~\cite{vaswani2017attentionIsAllYouNeed} encoder then
enriches these features through self-attention. A decoder takes a small fixed number of learnable positional
encodings, called object queries, and retrieves object information from the image features through cross-attention.
A prediction head then maps the resulting object embeddings to bounding box coordinates and class labels.
For training, DETR uses a bipartite matching strategy based on the Hungarian algorithm~\cite{ramshaw2012hungarianAlgorithm}.
This guarantees a unique one-to-one assignment between predictions and ground truth objects, so the model
learns to avoid duplicate detections. Compared to the proposal-based and YOLO families, DETR is therefore
fully end-to-end both in training and inference without NMS postprocessing, which can significantly impact CPU or GPU
compute~\cite{zhao2024DETRsBeatYolosRealTimeObjectDetection}.
However, DETR suffers from slow convergence, particularly for small objects.
Several follow-up works address these limitations.
DAB-DETR~\cite{liu2022DAB_DETR} improves upon DETR by introducing dynamic anchor boxes, which improve convergence
speed and small object detection. DINO~\cite{zhang2022DINO} builds on DETR with contrastive denoising and achieved
state-of-the-art detection accuracy, with the top-10 detection methods on the COCO~\cite{lin2014microsoftCOCO} dataset all being
transformer-based~\cite{zou2023objectDetectionSurvey}.
Beyond object detection, DETR also enabled a family of transformer-based end-to-end tracking
methods~\cite{zeng2022motr}, which are discussed further in Section~\ref{chap:tracking}.
Real-time optimised variants have since emerged, most notably RT-DETR~\cite{zhao2024DETRsBeatYolosRealTimeObjectDetection},
which achieves state-of-the-art real-time performance on the COCO dataset. This lead continues to grow with newer
versions and more training data, as demonstrated by RT-DETRv3~\cite{wang2025RT_DETRv3}.
This trend is also observed in RGB-thermal pedestrian and vehicle detection, where transformer-based methods
produce state-of-the-art accuracy, but are still slightly more computationally intensive than
single-stage detectors~\cite{guo2024damsdet, yuan2024c2former, kotthapalli2025yolov1Toyolov11}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/detr_architecture_carion_2020.png}
    \caption{
        The DETR architecture as a representative query-based detector \cite{carion2020DETR}.
    }
    \label{fig:detr_architecture}
\end{figure}

Despite these advances, even real-time optimised variants such as RT-DETR~\cite{zhao2024DETRsBeatYolosRealTimeObjectDetection} retain
the characteristic DETR weakness of inferior small object detection compared to the strongest real-time grid-based
detectors~\cite{zhao2024DETRsBeatYolosRealTimeObjectDetection}. This has also been observed in UAV-based thermal human detection,
where RT-DETR was outperformed by YOLO models~\cite{ulmuamei2025humanDetectionUAVThermalImagery}. The authors attribute
this to the nature of thermal datasets, which tend to be more heterogeneous and smaller than large-scale RGB benchmarks
such as COCO, exhibiting domain shifts across environments, altitudes, and sensor types that place transformer-based
detectors at a disadvantage because their attention mechanisms require larger and more consistent training data to
learn effectively~\cite{ulmuamei2025humanDetectionUAVThermalImagery}.

Beyond detection accuracy, deployment on the Hailo-8 NPU presents additional challenges for transformer-based
architectures. Hailo introduced DETR support in the Model Zoo and Dataflow Compiler in
2024~\cite{hailoModelZooChangelog2026}, and the Dataflow Compiler changelog notes parser and optimisation
improvements for transformer models with version 3.30 in 2025~\cite{hailoDFCChangelog2026}. Nevertheless,
performance on the Hailo-8 remains considerably lower than for CNN-based models, as the Hailo Model Explorer
reports significantly worse COCO object detection accuracy for DETR compared to YOLO models on this
hardware using the newest 3.33.1 compiler version for Hailo-8~\cite{hailoModelExplorer2026}. Researchers have also reported significant obstacles when deploying
transformer-based detectors on the Hailo-8, attributing them to the Dataflow Compiler being primarily optimised
for the static computational graphs of CNN-based architectures and struggling to map the dynamic graph
structure and complex attention mechanisms of transformers onto the fixed-function compute fabric of the
NPU~\cite{suchy2026reviewYOLODETREdgeDevices}. For these reasons, query-based detectors are found to be less
suitable for our application. Grid-based detectors are chosen for their better speed compared to proposal-based
detectors and very competitive accuracy.

\vspace{-2em}
\section{Multispectral Feature Fusion}
\vspace{-0.5em}

As outlined in Section~\ref{sec:sensor_selection}, visible light and thermal
infrared are used to exploit their complementary strengths. The fusion of these modalities
is the critical component for improved performance of the detection model.
The core problem in multispectral object detection is combining
the features from two different modalities, which can be described as
\begin{equation}
    F_{\text{Fused}} = F(F_R, F_T) = F(\phi_R(I_R), \phi_T(I_T)).
    \label{eq:fusion}
\end{equation}
$I_R$ and $I_T$ represent the input RGB and thermal images.
$F_R$ and $F_T$ denote the extracted feature maps for each modality,
obtained by applying the modality-specific feature extraction functions
$\phi_R(\cdot)$ and $\phi_T(\cdot)$. $F_{\text{Fused}}$ denotes the resulting
fused feature maps produced by the fusion function $F(\cdot)$.
Based on Eq.~(\ref{eq:fusion}), multi-modality fusion can be characterised
along two dimensions, namely the input features and the fusion function.
Accordingly, multi-modality fusion can be split into two levels,
selecting input features at the macro level, and constructing
the fusion function at the micro level.~\cite{qingyun2021CFT}

\vspace{-1em}
\subsection{Macro level feature fusion}

Macro-level fusion addresses the question of where in the network the
two modalities are combined, that is, at which feature stage fusion
is applied. The first study of
this kind~\cite{wagner2016multispectralDetectionDeepFusion} investigates
early fusion and late fusion and analyzes their
performance on multispectral data. Liu et al.~\cite{liu2016multispectralDeepNeuralNetworks}
extended this to mid fusion and score fusion to explore the
potential of deep models for multispectral pedestrian detection further.

The macro level fusion architectures are two-branch CNN models that perform
fusions at different feature stages. Early, mid and late fusion implement
feature fusions, as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:macro_fusion_architecture}, while score fusion
combines confidence scores from almost entirely separate color and
thermal models at the decision stage using NMS or averaging of the
respective detected bounding boxes.
\cite{liu2016multispectralDeepNeuralNetworks,li2019illuminationAwareFasterRCNN, wan2025yolov11rgbt}
Early Fusion concatenates the feature maps from color and thermal branches
immediately after the first convolutional layers, or concatenates the raw
modalities before the network, a variant known as input fusion. Input fusion
is the early fusion architecture used in this work.
Since these layers capture low-level visual features such as corners
and line segments, Early Fusion operates at a low semantic level.
Early fusion is therefore particularly susceptible to spatial
misalignment between the modalities, as misregistration in
image-level fused inputs is more difficult to correct than in
dual-branch architectures that process each modality
separately first~\cite{he2023misalignedRGBTadaptiveCalibration}.
Mid Fusion places the fusion module at the end of the feature extraction
backbone at the last convolutional layers. The features at this stage
carry more semantic meaning than in early fusion while retaining some
spatial detail.
Late Fusion fuses high-level features. In earlier CNN architectures
this occurred at the fully connected layers, whereas in modern detection
models it typically happens at the neck, after each modality has
independently completed its feature extraction at a stage that yields
robust object representations.
Score Fusion can be regarded as an ensemble of two CNNs that combines
detection scores from each modality through weighted averaging or
maximum selection to produce the final results.
\cite{liu2016multispectralDeepNeuralNetworks, li2019illuminationAwareFasterRCNN}


\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/macro_fusion_architecture.png}
    \caption{
        Macro-level fusion architectures for multispectral object detection.
        Adapted from \cite{wan2025yolov11rgbt}.
    }
    \label{fig:macro_fusion_architecture}
\end{figure}

Liu et al.~\cite{liu2016multispectralDeepNeuralNetworks} demonstrated that the mid fusion model
achieved the best detection performance. Subsequent
works~\cite{li2019illuminationAwareFasterRCNN, zhang2019crossModalityInteractiveAttentionNetwork}
validated that mid-level fusion outperforms the other fusion architectures,
and it has since been widely adopted and accepted as the best fusion stage for
multispectral object detection in modern
work~\cite{zhang2021GAFF,qingyun2021CFT, gallagher2025surveyMultispectralYOLO, wan2025yolov11rgbt}.
However, these works focus only on full-precision models, and the performance
of the different fusion architectures for quantized models has not been explored.
While previous work investigates RGB-thermal fusion for quantized models targeting
real-time edge deployment, it neglects comparing the performance of different macro
fusion architectures and relies exclusively on early
fusion~\cite{kafka2025EarlyFusionQuantizedModels}.
This work therefore evaluates all four fusion architectures and compares their
performance on a vehicle and pedestrian detection dataset for both full-precision
and quantized models in a real-time edge deployment scenario.

\vspace{-1em}
\subsection{Micro level feature fusion}
\label{sec:micro_fusion}

Besides the position of fusion defined in macro network architectures, the
construction of the fusion function $F(\cdot)$ is another key factor for
complementary learning of modality interaction.
The objective is to design $F(\cdot)$ such that complementary information
from both modalities is maximized, spatial misalignment and
modality-specific noise are mitigated, and the features most relevant
to the downstream task are retained for each modality.

Many strategies for the instantiation of the fusion function
$F(F_R, F_T)$ from Eq.~(\ref{eq:fusion}) have been explored, distinguished by the complexity of the learned operator $F(\cdot)$.
The simplest operators apply a fixed combination at every spatial location, including element-wise addition ($F_{\text{Fused}} = F_R + F_T$),
element-wise average ($F_{\text{Fused}} = \frac{1}{2}(F_R + F_T)$), element-wise maximum ($F_{\text{Fused}} = \max(F_R, F_T)$),
and element-wise multiplication ($F_{\text{Fused}} = F_R \odot F_T$).
Commonly, concatenation followed by a $1{\times}1$ convolution extends this to a learned linear mixing across
all input channels~\cite{liu2016multispectralDeepNeuralNetworks}.
The Network-in-Network (NIN)~\cite{lin2013networkInNetwork} concept replaces this single linear layer with a
small sub-network capable of modeling non-linear cross-modal interactions,
making it a widely adopted building block in multispectral fusion
modules~\cite{zhang2019crossModalityInteractiveAttentionNetwork, zhang2021GAFF, liang2023explicitAttentionEnhancedFusion, qingyun2021CFT}.
These operators are fast and parameter-efficient but treat both modalities equally regardless
of conditions, making them vulnerable when one modality is degraded by poor lighting or sensor noise.

Gating approaches replace fixed weights with learned, input-dependent masks.
A typical gating unit produces a weight map $g = \sigma\!\left(\mathrm{Conv}([F_R, F_T])\right)$
and blends the modalities as $g \odot F_R + (1 - g) \odot F_T$, where each pixel or channel is
weighted independently without any notion of spatial competition.\cite{zheng2019GFD_SSD, kim2018DeepGatedFusionNetwork}
Illumination-aware fusion is a prominent specialization of conditioned gating in which a dedicated deep network
estimates an illumination value from the color image and uses it to derive the gate.
Because the thermal modality is largely insensitive to lighting changes, the gate suppresses the
RGB branch under low-light conditions and places more weight on the thermal branch, as implemented
in the Illumination-Aware Network of Li et al.\ \cite{li2019illuminationAwareFasterRCNN}.
Illumination-aware gating outperforms fixed operators and linear mixing, with multispectral pedestrian
benchmarks such as KAIST showing gains from suppressing the RGB branch at night.
However, it is limited to a single weight per modality, with no spatial or channel granularity.

Pre-transformer spatial attention mechanisms refine gating by computing input-dependent element-wise weights
along either the channel or the spatial dimension, but remain local in scope.
The receptive field of the underlying convolution operator bounds the region over which each weight is
influenced, so distant features have no direct effect on one another.
Channel attention collapses the spatial dimensions of a feature map via global average or max
pooling to obtain a compact channel descriptor, which an MLP then maps to per-channel
scaling weights.
In the multispectral setting these mechanisms can be applied within a single modality
(intra-modal attention) or across modalities, where the channel or spatial statistics of
$F_T$ inform the weighting applied to $F_R$ and vice versa (inter-modal attention),
enabling selective emphasis of features that are complementary across the two input streams.
Attention-based methods outperform gating on KAIST and FLIR.
\cite{wang2020ECANet, zhang2019crossModalityInteractiveAttentionNetwork, zhang2021GAFF, liang2023explicitAttentionEnhancedFusion}
Transformer-based fusion \cite{qingyun2021CFT} removes the locality constraint entirely through
query-key-value cross-attention, which computes pairwise similarities between all spatial tokens
of $F_R$ and $F_T$ simultaneously, enabling long-range interactions that are beyond the reach of
convolutional attention.
Transformer cross-attention achieves the highest benchmark accuracy~\cite{qingyun2021CFT} but
its quadratic token complexity makes it considerably more expensive than convolutional designs.

Three micro-fusion variants are explored in this work, all applied at the mid-fusion
position of the YOLOv11 backbone.
The first, MidP3, restricts cross-modal feature combination to the first feature
pyramid scale shown in Figure~\ref{fig:grid_based_architecture} rather than all
three detection scales.
Multi-level feature fusion can cause redundancy in aligned multispectral datasets,
and restricting fusion to a single scale may allow for model size reduction
without performance loss~\cite{wan2025yolov11rgbt}.
The second is the cross-modality fusion transformer
(CFT)~\cite{qingyun2021CFT}, applied at all three detection scales and referred
to as midCFT3 throughout this work.
CFT fuses both modalities by downsampling each feature map to $8{\times}8$
patches via global average pooling.
The 128 concatenated tokens are passed to a transformer self-attention block, and the
attended output is upsampled by bilinear interpolation and added back to each
modality branch.
The two resulting modality branches are then combined by element-wise addition
to produce the fused feature map.
The third is the mixed local channel attention module
(MLCA)~\cite{wan2023MLCA}, applied at all three detection scales
and referred to as midCMA3 throughout this work.
MLCA computes pre-transformer channel attention weights from two pooled representations of each
feature map.
A local representation is obtained by average pooling to a $5{\times}5$ spatial
resolution, which retains spatially distributed channel statistics.
A global representation pools each channel to a single scalar which creates a complementary,
spatially agnostic view of overall channel importance.
A one-dimensional convolution is applied to each representation to model
inter-channel dependencies.
The resulting attention maps are summed and multiplied onto the feature map to
produce the fused output.

Regardless of the chosen object detection strategy, the detection pipeline described in this chapter
delivers a set of bounding boxes per frame together with their class and confidence scores.
For the downstream task of traffic monitoring, this per-frame view alone is insufficient.
Determining whether a vehicle is stationary or slow-moving requires observing the same vehicle
across consecutive frames and associating detections over time.
This temporal association problem is the subject of the following chapter.

\chapter{Multi-Object Tracking}
\label{chap:tracking}

Multi-object tracking (MOT) is a computer vision task that involves detecting and tracking objects
of a given category in a video. It operates without prior knowledge of the objects' appearance or their
location. Compared to object detection applied to a video, it additionally requires associating detections
across frames by assigning them unique object IDs. Solving MOT results in a set of tracks, where each
track consists of all detections belonging to the same object. The main difficulty arises from frequent
occlusions, where MOT algorithms,
i.e. trackers, may switch object IDs or lose track of some objects. Additionally, objects with similar
appearances make it difficult to distinguish between them, while unpredictable dynamic motion further
complicates tracking.
There are two major paradigms for online MOT, namely tracking-by-detection
and end-to-end tracking.

\vspace{-2em}
\section{Tracking-by-Detection}
\label{sec:tracking_by_detection}
\vspace{-0.5em}

The emergence of deep learning-based
methods led to introduction of tracking-by-detection frameworks.
Tracking-by-detection is a simple and highly efficient paradigm in MOT. The object detection and association
are decoupled and performed in independent steps. First, an object
detection model detects objects in each frame. Then, an association
method is used to associate objects across frames. This paradigm is widely adopted due to its modularity,
which enables independent optimization of detection and association modules, its flexibility to integrate
both traditional algorithms and deep learning models, and its good performance in modern applications.
Each component, such as object detector, motion model, association, can be replaced independently,
making it convenient
to adapt to different object detectors and the use of different association methods, both with
traditional and deep learning approaches. The tracking-by-detection methods evaluated in the tracker
evaluation section are introduced in the following paragraphs.
\cite{advzemovic2025deepLearningMultiObjectTrackingSurvey}

SORT (Simple Online and Real-time Tracking)~\cite{bewley2016SORT} is the foundational tracking-by-detection algorithm
and operates in three steps per frame. First, an object detector produces bounding box detections
filtered by a confidence threshold. Second, a Kalman Filter predicts each active track's next
bounding box position using a linear constant velocity motion model, whose state captures the
bounding box center, scale, aspect ratio, and their velocities. These predictions are then matched
to the current frame's detections via the Hungarian algorithm, which minimises assignment cost over
a matrix of pairwise IoU distances and rejects any pairing whose IoU falls below a minimum gate
threshold. Confirmed pairs update the Kalman Filter state via Bayesian inference, blending the
motion prior with the detection measurement. Third, unmatched detections are initialised as
candidate tracks that must survive a short probationary period of 3 consecutive associations
b default before receiving a confirmed identity, while confirmed tracks that remain unmatched for more
than $T_{lost}$ consecutive frames are deleted. With the default setting of $T_{lost} = 1$, SORT
does not tolerate long occlusions, which is one of its main limitations.
\cite{advzemovic2025deepLearningMultiObjectTrackingSurvey}

Deep SORT~\cite{wojke2017DeepSORT} targets SORT's tendency to accumulate identity switches during occlusions by augmenting
motion-based association with appearance information. An interchangeable convolutional re-identification model extracts
a compact embedding from each detected crop, and each track maintains a rolling buffer
of such embeddings. Similarity is measured as the minimum cosine distance
between the detection's embedding and any embedding in the track's buffer.
The final association cost blends this
appearance distance with the computed distance from the Kalman Filter prediction, with a gate that
requires both motion and appearance conditions to be satisfied before a pairing is considered.
Deep SORT also introduces cascaded association ordered by track age, so recently
active tracks are matched first and accumulated Kalman Filter uncertainty cannot cause lost tracks to
steal detections from visible objects. Confirmed tracks are prioritised over probationary candidates,
reducing false-positive associations.
\cite{advzemovic2025deepLearningMultiObjectTrackingSurvey}

ByteTrack~\cite{zhang2022bytetrack} builds directly on SORT and addresses its sensitivity to the detection score threshold
through a two-stage association strategy called Byte. In the first stage, high-confidence detections are
matched to all active and lost tracks using IoU similarity, optionally extended with appearance embeddings
from a re-identification model. In the second stage, the low-confidence detections that remain are matched
to the still-unmatched active tracks using IoU similarity alone, without appearance features, because
occluded objects tend to produce blurry, unreliable crops that would degrade re-identification quality.
This design reflects the key insight that low-confidence detections frequently correspond to partially
occluded objects rather than detector errors, so discarding them entirely, as SORT does, causes
unnecessary identity switches. A third association stage handles candidate tracks in their probationary
period by attempting to match them against any high-confidence detections not consumed in the first stage.
ByteTrack pairs this association pipeline with the YOLOX detector and has become the de-facto baseline for
the MOT benchmarks and serves as the foundation for a large family of subsequent tracking methods.
\cite{advzemovic2025deepLearningMultiObjectTrackingSurvey}

BoT-SORT~\cite{aharon2022BOTSORT} combines ByteTrack's two-stage association pipeline with several improvements borrowed
from or inspired by Deep SORT. The Kalman Filter adopts adaptive, time-dependent process and measurement
noise that scale with the tracked object's bounding box dimensions, making predictions more confident
for small, distant objects that are assumed to move less between frames. BoT-SORT introduces a camera
motion compensation (CMC) method to correct KF predictions between
frames in non-static camera scenes by estimating the camera motion through global image features.
In addition the re-identification backbone is upgraded to a
stronger appearance model. The association cost is also revised to incorporate appearance similarity
only when a track and detection are already in close spatial proximity, falling back to motion alone otherwise.

OC-SORT~\cite{cao2023OCSORT} addresses a fundamental weakness of the Kalman Filter shared by SORT, Deep SORT, and
ByteTrack, namely that prediction errors accumulate during occlusions and corrupt the track state
even after a lost object reappears. Its central contribution is observation-centric re-update (ORU),
which triggers a retrospective correction whenever a lost track is successfully re-associated after
$N$ missed frames. Rather than accepting the drifted Kalman Filter state, ORU interpolates the
missing bounding boxes across those N frames and replays the filter updates along the reconstructed
trajectory, resetting accumulated error. Two additional heuristics complement ORU. Observation-centric
momentum (OCM) augments the association cost with a motion consistency term derived from the
track's recent detection history, favouring matches that preserve the track's observed movement
pattern over those that only satisfy the Kalman Filter prediction. Observation-centric recovery
(OCR) adds a final association pass for still-unmatched tracks, using their last confirmed
detection position instead of the increasingly uncertain Kalman Filter extrapolation as the
reference bounding box. Together these mechanisms make OC-SORT a strong alternative to ByteTrack
in scenarios involving long occlusions and erratic motion, where linear constant-velocity assumptions
break down.

\vspace{-2em}
\section{End-to-End Tracking}
\label{sec:end_to_end_tracking}
\vspace{-0.5em}

In end-to-end tracking methods, object detection and association are trained jointly rather than as separate stages.
The most well-known methods of this type~\cite{zeng2022motr, zhang2023motrv2, yu2023motrv3, cai2022memot, gao2023memotr} build on the DETR
object detection model and its extension to tracking called MOTR
(Multi-Object Tracking with Transformer)~\cite{zeng2022motr}.
Since end-to-end methods learn to track directly from data, they offer a generalizable approach that is not domain-specific.
However, they come with significant computational demands and are slow during inference, which makes tracking-by-detection methods
generally more suitable for real-time applications. Furthermore, their transformer-based architecture makes them incompatible with
the Hailo-8 NPU due to hardware limitations, as already discussed in Chapter~\ref{chap:object_detection}.
\cite{advzemovic2025deepLearningMultiObjectTrackingSurvey}

\vspace{-2em}
\section{Tracking evaluation metrics}
\label{sec:tracking_evaluation_metrics}
\vspace{-0.5em}

Evaluating a multi-object tracker requires comparing predicted tracks against ground truth
tracks. Errors fall into two broad
categories. Association errors include false positive tracks (a tracked object does not exist in the
scene), false negative tracks (an existing object is never recognised throughout the video), and
identity switches (two tracks swap their detection assignments). Detection errors include false positive
detections, false negative detections, and localisation errors, where the predicted bounding box does
not precisely align with the ground truth. Figure~\ref{fig:mot_error_types} illustrates each of these
outcomes.~\cite{advzemovic2025deepLearningMultiObjectTrackingSurvey}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{images/mot_tracking_errors_advzemovic_2025.png}
    \caption{
        Six representative tracking outcomes illustrating (a) a correct track, (b) a false positive track,
        (c) a false negative track, (d) a false negative detection, (e) a false positive detection,
        and (f) an identity switch. All cases exhibit localisation errors. 
        Modified after \cite{advzemovic2025deepLearningMultiObjectTrackingSurvey}.
    }
    \label{fig:mot_error_types}
\end{figure}

The standard choice similarity measure to match predictions against ground truth for
2D bounding box detections is Intersection over Union (IoU). A prediction is matched to
a ground truth when their IoU exceeds a fixed threshold, typically 0.5. Because multiple predictions
can overlap a single ground truth and vice versa, all candidate pairs form a bipartite graph that is
resolved with the Hungarian algorithm to yield the optimal one-to-one assignment.
\cite{advzemovic2025deepLearningMultiObjectTrackingSurvey}

MOTA (Multi-Object Tracking Accuracy)~\cite{bernardin2006MOTAMOTP} and MOTP (Multi-Object Tracking Precision)\cite{bernardin2006MOTAMOTP} are among the
earliest and most widely used MOT metrics~\cite{advzemovic2025deepLearningMultiObjectTrackingSurvey}. MOTA is defined as $\text{MOTA} = 1 - \frac{|\text{FP}| + |\text{FN}| + |\text{IDSW}|}{|\text{gtDet}|}$,
where $|\text{FP}|$, $|\text{FN}|$, and $|\text{IDSW}|$ are the total counts of false positive
detections, false negative detections, and identity switches, and $|\text{gtDet}|$ is the total number
of ground truth detections. Its values range from $-\infty$ to 1 with higher values indicating better
performance. MOTP is the average IoU over all correctly matched detection pairs and captures
localisation quality independently of the association errors that affect MOTA. Because false positive
and false negative detections dominate the MOTA sum in most practical scenarios, MOTA is primarily
sensitive to detection quality rather than association quality.


IDF1~\cite{ristani2016IDF1} addresses this imbalance by performing matching at the track level rather than at the detection
level and is defined as the harmonic mean of ID-Recall and ID-Precision. ID-Recall is the share of
ground truth identity matches that are correctly recovered relative to all ground truth matches.
ID-Precision is the share of predicted identity matches that are correct relative to all predicted
matches. IDF1 therefore complements MOTA by focusing on association accuracy and identity consistency.

HOTA (Higher Order Tracking Accuracy)~\cite{luiten2021HOTA} is the primary metric on current benchmarks because it
explicitly balances detection and association quality. It is defined as the geometric mean of a
detection score (DetA) and an association score (AssA). DetA measures how accurately the tracker
detects objects. It is computed as the intersection over union of the predicted and ground truth sets of
detections. The intersection is the number of true
positives and the union is the sum of true positives, false positives, and false negatives.
To reward both detection accuracy and localisation precision, DetA is averaged across IoU
thresholds from 0.05 to 0.95. AssA measures how consistently the tracker links those detections into tracks.
For every correctly matched ground truth and predicted track ID pair, it computes the fraction of
detections along the corresponding ground truth and predicted tracks that are consistently associated
with that same pair, and then averages this score over all matched pairs. By separating detection and association errors,
HOTA provides a more complete picture of tracker
performance than MOTA or IDF1 alone. Therefore, it became the primary metric in MOT benchmarks alongside
AssA, DetA, MOTA, IDF1, and the total number of identity switches~\cite{advzemovic2025deepLearningMultiObjectTrackingSurvey}.

\vspace{-2em}
\section{Evaluation}
\vspace{-0.5em}

For this application, we choose the tracking-by-detection paradigm for its modularity, and
better performance in real-time applications. To select a specific tracking-by-detection method,
the outlined trackers are evaluated on the 3D-Net dataset as outlined in Chapter~\ref{chap:dataset}.
In addition to the detection bounding boxes, object indices, which identify the objects across frames,
are added to the 3D-Net dataset, which are used as ground truth for the tracking evaluation.
Using ground truth detections as tracker input simulates a well-configured object detector and
ensures that the evaluation metrics reflect only the tracker's association and motion modeling
capabilities, without being distorted by the prediction behavior of any specific detector.
The dataset footage was recorded at 20 fps with every second frame skipped, resulting in an
effective evaluation frame rate of 6 fps. For trackers that expose a frame-rate parameter, this
was set to 6. The lost-track buffer was set to 30 frames for all trackers and the minimum number
of consecutive detections required to confirm a new track was set to 2, with all remaining
parameters left at their defaults.
Low frame rates are known to reduce tracking accuracy across all methods, with relative performance
remaining largely consistent across moderate frame rate reductions~\cite{mabrouk2022assessment}.

Comparing the two output modes, the Kalman filter predictions consistently reduce detection
accuracy DetA across all trackers while association accuracy and IDF1 scores remain largely
unchanged. The motion model therefore introduces positional error relative to the detector output
without improving identity consistency, therefore the detection bounding box output mode is used for the
final tracker. While deep learning-based motion model alternatives may yield better predictions in the
general case~\cite{sun2022dancetrack, xiao2024motiontrack, advzemovic2025beyondKalmanFilters}, they were not explored here as
the detection model is expected to deliver accurate localization in the target environment where occlusion is infrequent,
and they would add further computational load to the application.

The ReID modules of DeepSort and BoTSort with ReID show no measurable benefit in tracking accuracy
over the purely motion-based trackers on this dataset. ReID features are most effective in scenarios
involving long occlusions, frequent object crossings, or heavily overlapping trajectories, where
appearance cues are needed to re-establish identity after a track is lost~\cite{advzemovic2025deepLearningMultiObjectTrackingSurvey}.
In the target use case of vehicle tracking, objects follow relatively predictable trajectories
with little spatial overlap and few sustained occlusions, so motion-based association in
combination with a high-quality object detector is sufficient.
Neither ReID model was fine-tuned on the 3D-Net dataset, and domain-adapted appearance features
may improve the association accuracy of the respective trackers. Similarly, the camera motion compensation
module of BoT-SORT yields no measurable improvement.
Although the drone does move during some sequences, the object count during those periods is low
enough that motion-based association can still resolve matches without requiring camera motion
correction.

ByteTrack is selected as the tracker for this application. It achieves the lowest per-frame latency
while also achieving the best tracking accuracy across both evaluated output modes. Each tracker
encodes domain-specific heuristics targeting particular challenges such as long occlusions,
non-linear motion, or tracking distant objects, and ByteTrack's design best matches the
characteristics of this application with small relatively fast moving objects~\cite{advzemovic2025deepLearningMultiObjectTrackingSurvey}.

For the model training pipeline, we use the open-source MLOps platform ClearML to orchestrate and
monitor the training process. ClearML provides experiment tracking, remote task execution, dataset
versioning, and a web-based dashboard for visualizing metrics and comparing runs. It wraps existing

performance curves throughout each experiment. The training tasks are dispatched to GPU workers
hosted on Bernard's GPU Rack, which retrieve the training dataset from the S3 bucket through FiftyOne
at runtime. The workers use 10-core Intel Xeon Silver 4210 CPUs and NVIDIA RTX 2080 Ti GPUs with

0.01, a momentum of 0.937, and a weight decay of 0.0005. All models are trained for 300 epochs with
a batch size of 32 and an input resolution of 640x640 pixels. The single-modality YOLOv11n models are
initialized with weights pre-trained on the COCO dataset, while the multimodal RGBT fusion variants
are initialized by loading those same pretrained weights into the custom dual-branch architecture.
For data augmentation, we employ Mosaic augmentation, random horizontal flipping (p = 0.5), HSV
color-space jitter, and random scaling. The experiment logs, training artifacts, metrics, and debugging
samples with visualized detections as well as well prepared plots created in the training application
code are stored in the self-hosted ClearML server after each training run, which allows easy comparison
and analysis of different training runs through the ClearML web interface with organized storage.
Example views of the ClearML web interface for a training run are shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:clearml_dashboard}. All training code, including dataset loading, model definition, and
training loops, is implemented in Python, configuration, and environment files, that are built into
Docker images stored on an Azure container registry, where the ClearML workers execute the
corresponding task Docker container on the GPU Rack.

\section{Hailo Dataflow Compilation}
\vspace{-0.5em}

Deploying the trained network on the Hailo-8 cannot reuse standard
runtimes such as ONNX Runtime or TensorRT, because the accelerator is a
domain-specific neural processing unit whose compute fabric is built
around a set of statically scheduled clusters of multiply-accumulate
engines backed by a distributed on-chip memory, with no
general-purpose floating-point arithmetic unit and without a
connection to external DRAM~\cite{hailo8_m2datasheet2021,hailo8_productBrief2023}.
Every layer of the network must therefore be lowered into a
hardware-specific bitstream ahead of time, and every FP32 weight and
activation value must be translated into an 8-bit fixed-point
representation (or optionally a mix of 8-bit and 4-bit for weights)
that the Hailo compute engines process
natively~\cite{krishnamoorthi2018quantizingWhitepaper,hailo8_productBrief2023}.
This translation is carried out by Hailo's Dataflow Compiler, which
produces a \texttt{.hef} (Hailo Executable Format) file that is loaded
at runtime by the HailoRT library on the Raspberry Pi
host~\cite{hailoDataflowCompilerUserGuide}. Figure~\ref{fig:hailo_software_suite}
situates the compiler in Hailo's wider software stack and shows the
split between the Model Build Environment, which groups the machine
learning frameworks, the Hailo Model Zoo (a collection of pre-trained
and retrainable models in TensorFlow and ONNX), and the Dataflow
Compiler itself, and the Runtime Environment on the host processor,
which groups the HailoRT runtime that executes compiled models on the
Hailo-8 device over PCIe, and user applications built on top of
it~\cite{hailo8_productBrief2023}.

    \caption{
        Overview of the Hailo-8 software
        suite~\cite{hailo8_productBrief2023}.

\subsection{Pipeline Stages}

The Dataflow Compiler structures the conversion as three sequential
stages, namely Parsing (Translation), Optimization and Compilation as seen in
Figure~\ref{fig:hailo_software_suite}. Each stage
consumes a Hailo Archive (\texttt{.har}) and emits a transformed
\texttt{.har}, and the last stage writes the final \texttt{.hef}
executable. The stages are kept separate because their inputs and
their failure modes are different. Parsing only needs the operator
graph, Optimization additionally needs a calibration dataset, and
Compilation additionally needs the target hardware architecture, so
separating them lets the developer re-run the expensive later stages
without repeating the earlier
ones~\cite{hailoDataflowCompilerUserGuide}.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{images/hailo_software_suite.png}

        process.~\cite{hailo8_productBrief2023}.
    }
    \label{fig:hailo_build_process}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Parsing}

Parsing reads the ONNX operator graph produced by exporting the
trained model, verifies that every operator inside the model has a
supported Hailo equivalent (e.g. convolutions, average pooling, concatenation, etc.)
and converts the graph into the Hailo archive format (HAR) that
the later stages operate
on. For YOLO versions up to
YOLOv5 the dataflow compiler automatically executes non-maximum
suppression (NMS) on the Hailo-8 TPU core, whereas from YOLOv8
onwards the core no longer supports NMS directly and the operation
is instead performed on the host CPU as a post-processing step that
consumes the raw convolution outputs of the detection
head~\cite{hailoDataflowCompilerUserGuide}.

This arrangement dictates where the parser must cut the ONNX graph
of a YOLOv11n model. Each of the three detection scales of the head
ends in two parallel convolutions, one emitting a bounding-box
distribution and one emitting the per-class logits, and the ONNX
export decodes the distribution into actual box coordinates and
concatenates the boxes before the graph
output~\cite{hidayatullah2025yolov8toyolo11Review}. The auto-detected
end nodes therefore sit after this decoding, which conflicts with
the CPU post-processing step that would decode the distribution a
second time, and the parsing stage fails with mismatching layer
outputs. To obtain the correct
cut, the exported \texttt{.onnx} was opened in
Netron~\cite{netronViewer}, the detection head at \texttt{/model.23}
was inspected, and the last \texttt{Conv} node of the \texttt{cv2}
(box distribution) and \texttt{cv3} (class scores) branches at each
of the three scales was identified manually, yielding the six
end-node names that are passed to the
parser.~\cite{hailoDataflowCompilerUserGuide}

\subsection{Optimization}

The optimization stage decomposes into two phases. A full-precision
phase applies graph-level modifications to the parsed model in the
floating-point domain. These modifications express transformations the
ONNX export does not itself contain, such as input normalization or
the insertion of hardware post-processing blocks on supported
detection architectures that shift preprocessing like colour or format
conversion, input resize, and post-processing work like NMS from the
host CPU onto the accelerator. They also apply numerical equivalences
that make the network easier to optimize. A subsequent numerical phase
then carries out the actual quantization and accuracy recovery
described in the remainder of this
subsection.~\cite{hailoDataflowCompilerUserGuide}

A neural network trained in 32-bit floating point represents every
weight and every intermediate activation as a real number with about
seven decimal digits of precision. On edge hardware the memory
bandwidth, the on-chip storage, and the power budget for arithmetic
units are orders of magnitude more constrained than on a GPU, and
most of that precision is not actually used by the trained network.
Quantization replaces high-precision values with compact codes from a
smaller numerical type, which can be uniform with equally spaced
levels or non-uniform with levels concentrated where the value
distribution is denser. The target format ranges from FP16 through
INT16 and INT8 to INT4, and the transformation can be applied to an
already-trained network as post-training quantization or folded into
training as quantization-aware training.
The Hailo compiler fixes most of these choices to uniform affine
quantization with signed integers, replacing each floating-point
value with a compact integer code together with two layer-wide
parameters that recover the original scale. The default target is 8 bits for both weights and
activations, with 16 bits available as an opt-in for layers whose
accuracy is sensitive to precision loss and 4 bits available as an
opt-in for weights of bandwidth-limited layers. Scales for weights
are assigned per channel for finer control while activation scales are
shared across the full tensor, and both are determined statically from
a calibration run and frozen at inference time. The numerical phase
can stop after this PTQ step, or it can proceed at higher
optimization levels to an additional quantization-aware finetuning
(QFT) step, a form of quantization-aware training applied to an
already-quantized model. In this work the QFT step is enabled because
it yields better accuracy at the accepted cost of longer compile
time.~\cite{hailoQuantizationFineTuning,gholami2021surveyQuantization, hailoDataflowCompilerUserGuide, finkelstein2022qft}

Post-training quantization maps every floating-point value to an
8-bit signed integer through the affine map $x_q =
    \mathrm{round}(x / s) + z$, where $s$ is a per-layer scale factor
and $z$ is a zero point. The integer tensors are four times smaller
than their FP32 counterparts, fit into the Hailo on-chip memory
without spilling to DRAM, and the expensive floating-point
multiplications inside convolutions collapse into cheap integer
multiplications that the Hailo compute engines are optimized for.
The scale $s$ and zero point $z$ cannot be chosen analytically
because the distribution of activations at each layer depends on the
input data. The compiler therefore runs the unquantized FP32 network
on a representative calibration batch drawn from the training set,
records the minimum, maximum, and percentile statistics of every
intermediate tensor, and picks a scale that tightly covers the bulk
of the observed range while clipping extreme outliers that would
otherwise stretch the scale and waste integer codes on values that
almost never occur. The official recommendation for the calibration
set size is at least 1024 images. In this work we use
2048.~\cite{hailoDataflowCompilerUserGuide,hailoQuantizationFineTuning, gholami2021surveyQuantization}

Even with a well-chosen calibration, naively rounding every weight to
the nearest integer loses accuracy, typically several mAP points on
dense-prediction models such as YOLO, because the rounding error in
one layer feeds into the next and compounds along the
network~\cite{nagel2020adaround}. The Hailo compiler therefore runs
two label-free, gradient-free recovery algorithms as part of the PTQ
step. The first is iterative
bias correction after Finkelstein et al. 2019, which addresses the
fact that rounding is no longer unbiased once the activation
distribution of a channel is skewed, so that the mean of every
quantized channel drifts slightly away from the floating-point mean
and the drift compounds from layer to layer. The method estimates this
mean shift per channel by running the quantized network and the FP32
reference side by side on unlabeled calibration images, and folds the
estimate back into the layer bias so that the channel means of the
quantized network match those of the FP32 network, without any
gradient computation or labels~\cite{finkelstein2019ibc}. The second
is adaptive rounding (AdaRound) after Nagel et al. 2020, which starts
from the observation that rounding every weight to its nearest integer
minimises the per-weight error but not the per-layer output error,
because neighbouring weights interact through the convolution.
AdaRound therefore treats the rounding direction of every weight as a
learned binary variable and searches, per layer, for the combination
of up and down roundings that minimises the reconstruction error at
the layer output when the layer is fed calibration
activations~\cite{nagel2020adaround}.

Quantization-aware finetuning (QFT) after Finkelstein et al. 2022 is
the additional step that follows PTQ at higher optimization levels.
Unlike the two recovery algorithms above, QFT requires gradient
computation. It wraps the already-quantized model in a short training
loop that jointly tunes all continuous degrees of freedom of the
quantized network at once, namely the per-layer scales, the activation
clipping thresholds, and small residual corrections on the weights and
biases~\cite{finkelstein2022qft}. QFT uses the original FP32 model as
a teacher through knowledge
distillation~\cite{hinton2015knowledgeDistillation}, where the
teacher output serves as the training target for the quantized model.
Gradients propagate through the non-differentiable rounding step via
the Straight-Through Estimator, which on the backward pass treats the
rounding as the identity
function.~\cite{gholami2021surveyQuantization, hailoQuantizationFineTuning}

\vspace{-1em}
\subsection{Compilation}
\label{sec:hailo_compilation}

The final stage maps the quantized graph onto the physical Hailo-8
fabric, allocates each layer to one of the on-chip compute clusters,
schedules the flow of activations between clusters, and pre-places
intermediate tensors in on-chip memory so that inference never has
to wait on external DRAM.
The target-architecture flag of the compiler (\texttt{hailo8} or
\texttt{hailo8l}) must match the physical device, because the two
chips differ in their number of compute clusters, and using the
wrong flag produces a binary that will not load on the
hardware. Unusual network
topologies can cause the allocator to report an infeasible mapping
on the target architecture.~\cite{hailoDataflowCompilerUserGuide,hailo8_m2datasheet2021}

Three fusion architectures trained in this work could not be compiled for the
Hailo-8 due to unsupported operations.
The midCMA3 model uses the MLCA attention module, which reshapes feature tensors
from four dimensions to three to pass them through a one-dimensional convolution
before reshaping back.
The Hailo-8 operates exclusively on four-dimensional NHWC tensors and rejects
any change in tensor rank with an
\texttt{UnsupportedShuffleLayerError}~\cite{hailo_shuffle_reshape_5850,hailo_reshape_bchw_14560}.
The module additionally applies a channel-wise max-pooling operation with an
axis remapping not supported by the compiler, yielding an
\texttt{UnsupportedReduceMaxLayerError}.
The midCFT3 variant fails with a \texttt{FeatureSplitterLayerError} and an
\texttt{UnsupportedShuffleLayerError}, which is consistent with the limited
transformer support reported by other researchers and documented in the official
Hailo community forums~\cite{suchy2026reviewYOLODETREdgeDevices,hailo_multihead_attention_968}.
The score fusion model avoids operation-level errors but uses six detection
heads, three per modality, rather than the three heads used by the other models.
The allocator cannot map this topology to the available compute clusters.
None of the three architectures were modified to enable compilation, as
establishing equivalence to the original implementations would require separate
validation.

\vspace{-1em}
\subsection{Model Script}

The default behaviour of each pipeline stage can be overridden
through an optional plain-text Hailo model script with the extension
\texttt{.alls}, which is loaded at the Optimization or Compilation
entry points and dispatches its commands to the phase they apply to,
namely full-precision graph modifications, numerical quantization
tuning and compilation-stage allocator
hints. Two full-precision
modifications were required for the network trained in this project.
First, because the detector accepts a four-channel input stacking
three RGB channels with one thermal channel rather than the
three-channel input for which the Dataflow Compiler auto-generates a
normalization statement, the normalization command is rewritten in
four-element form \texttt{normalization([0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0], [255.0,
            255.0, 255.0, 255.0])}, which lowers the division by 255 into the
accelerator hardware so that the on-chip engines receive the same
$[0, 1]$ inputs the network was trained
on. Second, the three
class-score output convolutions at the three detection scales are
wrapped by a \texttt{change\_output\_activation($\ldots$, sigmoid)}
command, because the ONNX model was cut to end at the convolution layers,
therefore exporting the class logits instead of the class scores,
while Hailo's non-maximum-suppression
block expects the class scores in $[0, 1]$ at its
input~\cite{hailoDataflowCompilerUserGuide}.

Beyond these two modifications, the full-precision phase can insert
additional graph modifications such as colour or format conversion,
input resize and hardware post-processing on supported
architectures, to shift more work off the host
CPU. The numerical phase is
controlled by two main knobs, namely the optimization level, which
sets how aggressive the post-quantization recovery algorithms run
and therefore trades compile time against accuracy, and the
compression level, which sets the fraction of weights that are
compressed to 4 bits and therefore trades inference throughput
against the accuracy burden placed on the recovery
passes. At compilation time, a
performance mode can be enabled that maximises the utilisation of
the on-chip compute clusters and therefore the inference throughput
of the compiled binary at the cost of a longer compile
time.~\cite{hailoDataflowCompilerUserGuide} All models built in this
work were compiled with the highest optimization level of 4, no
weight compression and the highest compilation level of 2 to
prioritise accuracy over compile time.

\subsection{Execution Environment}

The Dataflow Compiler is only released for x86 Linux hosts, which
rules out running it on the Raspberry Pi itself, and a full run at
the highest optimization level takes several hours on a GPU worker,
so the pipeline is wrapped as a ClearML child task downstream of the
training job described in the previous subsection and produces the
\texttt{.hef} artifact that is consumed by the on-board HailoRT runtime.
~\cite{hailoDataflowCompilerUserGuide}
The compiled model is loaded on the Raspberry Pi host to run the
vehicle tracking service, which is further outlined in the next chapter.

\vspace{-2em}
\section{Vehicle Tracking Service}
\vspace{-0.5em}

The vehicle tracking service is implemented in Python and uses the natively
installed HailoRT library to execute the compiled model on the Hailo-8.
The service runs as a Docker container to provide a reproducible deployment
environment. The HailoRT runtime is attached as a Docker volume to keep the
image build process short and the resulting image small.

The service is structured around a central inference service that processes
incoming video frames from the RGB and thermal cameras.
The general information flow of the service is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:detection_service_flow}.
Frame acquisition is handled by two stream capture instances, one per camera
stream, that abstract the RTSP streams and run in separate threads to prevent
the main inference loop from blocking.
Thread isolation per stream also prevents frame corruption that arises when a
single process cannot keep up with the combined frame rate of two simultaneous
streams.
This issue occurred during the first data collection session and produced
corrupted frames that required removal during dataset curation.
Each stream capture instance maintains a thread-safe double-ended queue of
recent frames from which the latest pair can be matched by timestamp.
The live frame provider reads these timestamp-matched frame pairs from both
queues to ensure that inference operates on temporally aligned RGB and thermal
frames.
Before forwarding the pair to the inference service, the live frame provider
applies the alignment transform to register the thermal frame to the RGB frame
as described in Section~\ref{chap:sensor_alignment}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/detection_service_flow_nunito_large.png}
    \caption{
        The information flow of the vehicle tracking service with the major classes and their interactions.
    }
    \label{fig:detection_service_flow}
\end{figure}

On receiving an aligned frame pair, the inference service normalizes pixel
values and applies letterbox resizing to match the model input format before
passing the frames through the main inference loop.
After on-device inference, a post-processing step applies additional NMS with
confidence filtering above the threshold set in the Hailo model configuration.
This avoids model recompilation when the confidence threshold needs to be
adjusted.
The IoU threshold for NMS is applied entirely on the Hailo and is embedded in
the model script, so it requires recompilation to change.
After NMS, the detections are forwarded to the tracker, which assigns
consistent object IDs across frames for use by downstream congestion and
traffic flow analysis applications.
The tracker output is published via a ZeroMQ publisher to a message broker,
which decouples the tracking service from downstream consumers.

The service includes a performance monitor that collects per-frame diagnostics
from the inference service alongside hardware resource usage metrics from the
Raspberry Pi host and the Hailo-8 NPU.
Per-frame diagnostics include frame pair timestamp drift, execution times for
frame alignment, preprocessing, inference, post-processing, and tracking, as
well as the list of detected objects with their class labels, confidence
scores, and bounding box coordinates.
Hardware metrics include CPU and Hailo device temperatures, CPU utilization,
memory usage, and Hailo device utilization.
To read Hailo-8 performance metrics, a parser reads binary data written by the
HailoRT scheduler to \texttt{/tmp/hmon\_files}, which is mounted as a Docker
volume to expose the metrics to the containerized service.
The binary files are decoded using Protocol Buffers with the schema defined in
\texttt{scheduler\_mon.proto}, sourced from the open-source HailoRT
repository~\cite{hailort_github}.
All diagnostics are written to CSV files for offline analysis and are also
displayed in real time on the console.

The power consumption of the service cannot be measured using software tools
on the Raspberry Pi host.
The Raspberry Pi 5 includes a PMIC that reports voltages and currents for 12
internal power branches, but several power lines supplying USB devices, HATs,
and NVMe storage are not routed through the PMIC, so total board power
consumption is not available.
The Raspberry Pi AI HAT+ product brief~\cite{raspberryPiAiHatdatasheet2026}
lists no INA-series current monitoring circuits, and the Hailo-8 platform
device control interface reports current monitoring as
disabled~\cite{hailo_forum_current_monitoring2024}.
Therefore, an external power supply is used to measure the power consumption
of the system during inference in Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}.

The tracking service is designed to be modular and configurable through a YAML
configuration file, so that key components can be swapped or tuned without
code changes.
The frame provider interface abstracts the source of input frames and is
implemented by a live camera provider as well as by dataset-specific providers
for each evaluation dataset, which allows the service to run on recorded video
for testing and evaluation.
Camera settings such as frame rate, IP address, and RTSP timestamp offset for
both streams are defined in the configuration file, as are the alignment
transform parameters described in Section~\ref{chap:sensor_alignment} and the
ZeroMQ endpoint for publishing detection results.
The tracker type is also configurable; supported options are SORT, ByteTrack,
and OCSORT, all of which run on the CPU to assign consistent object IDs across
frames.
Two capture modes are configurable per stream: raw stream capture and
detection recording, where the latter stores the aligned frame video with or
without detection overlays.
Capture can also be disabled entirely.
The hardware resource poll rate in the monitoring configuration was set to
every 30 frames to avoid performance degradation from frequent I/O polling.

\section{Detection Accuracy}
\vspace{-0.5em}

The primary measurement used for determining the detection accuracy of the trained
models is the mean average precision (mAP). The mAP is computed by first calculating
the average precision (AP) per class, which summarises the area under the
precision-recall curve, and then averaging those per-class values across all classes.
Precision and recall are defined in terms of true positives,
false positives, and false negatives, where a predicted bounding box counts as a true
positive only if its intersection over union (IoU) with the matching ground-truth box
meets or exceeds a given threshold $t$. The IoU measures the ratio of the overlapping
area to the combined area of the predicted and ground-truth boxes, with
$\text{IoU} \geq t$ indicating a correct detection.
\cite{padilla2020surveyPerformanceMetricsObjectDetection}

Two variants of this threshold are used throughout this evaluation. The first is
mAP@50, which applies a fixed IoU threshold of $t = 0.50$ and thus rewards detectors
that localise objects approximately. The second is mAP50:95, which averages mAP
values computed at ten IoU thresholds ranging from $0.50$ to $0.95$ in steps of $0.05$
and therefore penalises imprecise bounding-box localisation.
\cite{padilla2020surveyPerformanceMetricsObjectDetection}

The object detectors evaluated in this section are the single-modality RGB and
thermal models and the different macro fusion approaches of early, mid,
late, and score fusion, which concatenate the modalities at
different stages as described in
Chapter~\ref{chap:object_detection}. In addition, the micro-fusion variants MidP3, midCFT3, and midCMA3 applied at
the mid-fusion position are evaluated, as described in
Section~\ref{sec:micro_fusion}.

The object detector performance on the dataset validation sets is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:fusion_performance_full_precision}. Despite the vast differences
in model parameter count and computational cost in terms of floating-point operations
(FLOPS) seen in Figure~\ref{fig:model_complexity}, the detection performances
are relatively equal in most cases.
The model performances very high for our 3D-Net dataset, which is because
the dataset is relatively easy and only has clear daytime images with good visibility
and a low amount of occlusions with a small number of objects per frame.
The most similar dataset to our 3D-Net dataset is the RTDOD dataset in terms of
perspective and objects, includes more challengin conditions such as rain, fog, evening,
and night conditions with denser object distributions resulting in lower performance.
What is notable is that for both the RTDOD and the 3D-Net datasets, the
thermal modality performs significantly worse than the rest, while for the M3FD and
Visdrone Dronevehicle datasets the thermal modality performs better or equal to the rest.
The reason for this is that the RTDOD and 3D-Net datasets have more spatio-temporal
misalignment between the RGB and thermal modalities than the other two datasets and
the RGB labels are used for evaluation.
For the larger combined dataset, the performance trends show that the performance tends to
increase with increasing model size and later fusion, while this trend is
not clear from the individual datasets.
Exact numerical results are listed in Table~\ref{tab:acc_full_precision}
in Appendix~\ref{chap:detection_accuracy_tables}.

To evaluate the performance of the models on different object sizes, the mAP metrics are
computed separately for small, medium, and large objects. Here we adopt the most common
object size classification from the COCO dataset into small, medium and large objects.
The classification is based on the area of the object bounding box, where small
objects have less than $32^2$ pixels, medium objects range from $32^2$ to $96^2$ pixels,
and large objects have an area of more than $96^2$ pixels.
~\cite{lin2014microsoftCOCO, kisantal2019augmentationSmallObjectDetection, chen2020fourPillarsSmallObjectDetectionSurvey}

The per-size mAP computation begins with a global IoU-based matching step that assigns
each prediction to at most one ground-truth box. This one-to-one assignment is resolved
before any size bin is considered. A matched prediction is then attributed to the size
bin of the ground-truth box it was assigned to. If that ground-truth box falls in the
small, medium, or large area range, the prediction is counted as a true positive for
that bin, regardless of the area of the predicted bounding box. Predictions that remain
unmatched after the global step are attributed to the size bin whose area range contains
their predicted bounding-box area and are counted as false positives. Ground-truth boxes
that are never matched to a prediction are counted as false negatives in their respective
size bin.

Figure~\ref{fig:model_performance_by_size} shows the detection performance on the 3D-Net dataset
for models trained on the 3D-Net dataset alone and the combined dataset, reported separately
for small, medium, and large objects. Performance on small objects is lower than on medium
and large objects across all models. This is a known trend in object detection, as small
objects provide limited visual information for the
detector~\cite{kisantal2019augmentationSmallObjectDetection, chen2020fourPillarsSmallObjectDetectionSurvey}.
The thermal modality achieves similar mAP50 on large objects as the RGB and fusion models,
but shows lower mAP50:95. This precision gap arises because the RGB modality was used
for annotation and the two modalities are not perfectly aligned, so a correct thermal
detection appears geometrically imprecise when evaluated against RGB labels.
On small and medium objects, the thermal modality performs significantly worse than RGB,
as object features are less distinguishable in the thermal domain. On large and medium
objects, the RGB and fusion models achieve comparable performance. On small objects, the
fusion models perform significantly better than the RGB model, which demonstrates the
benefit of the additional thermal information for object detection.

The transition to the combined dataset does not impact performance on large and medium
objects significantly and slightly improves results for some models on large objects.
On small objects, however, the performance advantage of the fusion models over the RGB
model disappears. The additional datasets were captured with
thermal cameras of different types and post-processing pipelines. Thermal cameras vary
more widely than RGB cameras in their sensing technology and signal processing. This
result hints that the
camera variability hinders the detection of camera-specific fine-grained thermal
features needed for the fusion advantage on small objects.

The micro-fusion variants midCMA3 and midCFT3 show small performance improvements over
the standard mid-fusion across all object sizes. The improvement is most visible in the
small object category for models trained on the combined dataset. These variants do not,
however, reproduce the thermal advantage over RGB that the standard early, mid, and late
fusion models achieve. The midCMA3 and midCFT3 data-pool models outperform the standard
mid-fusion model in the small object category, but remain below the late fusion model.
Exact numerical results are listed in Table~\ref{tab:acc_3dnet_size}
in Appendix~\ref{chap:detection_accuracy_tables}.

The KAT drone is equipped with two 6S LiPo (lithium polymer) batteries with a capacity of
12{,}000\,mAh each, where 6S denotes six lithium polymer cells connected in series, with a
single cell having a nominal voltage of 3.7\,V, yielding a nominal pack voltage of
$6 \times 3.7\,\text{V} = 22.2\,\text{V}$. Connected in parallel, the two packs provide a
combined energy of $2 \times 12\,\text{Ah} \times 22.2\,\text{V} \approx 533\,\text{Wh}$.
With a measured in-flight power draw of 1{,}600\,W, the theoretical maximum flight time is
$533\,\text{Wh} / 1{,}600\,\text{W} \approx 20\,\text{min}$, reducing to roughly
$0.75 \times 20\,\text{min} \approx 15\,\text{min}$ when accounting for the recommended
75\,\% usable capacity limit of LiPo cells. The 5\,W power draw of the Raspberry~Pi~5 running the tracking service
represents $5\,\text{W} / 1{,}600\,\text{W} \approx 0.3\,\%$ of the total system power
consumption, corresponding to a flight time reduction of under 3 seconds and is therefore
negligible.

\chapter{Limitations}
\label{chap:limitations}

The collected 3D-Net dataset is relatively small and restricted to clear daytime conditions
at a single road segment, due to regulatory constraints on drone flights. The dataset
contains few occlusion scenarios, and no traffic congestion phenomena are captured. This
limits the ability to evaluate downstream models for congestion and traffic flow analysis
within the 3D-Net project. Further data collection sessions are needed.

Adverse weather conditions, night scenes, and geographic diversity are partially addressed through the
public RGB-thermal datasets DroneVehicle, RTDOD, and M3FD. However, these datasets were
collected with different sensors, at different altitudes, and from different viewing angles
than the 3D-Net configuration. The resulting domain gap introduces uncertainty about
whether the detection performance measured on the combined training distribution translates
to the actual sensor setup in operational conditions.

All fusion variants share the same YOLOv11n base architecture, which makes the conclusions about
fusion strategy effectiveness specific to this detector family. Alternative base
detectors are not evaluated. Three of the evaluated fusion architectures (midCFT3, midCMA3,
and score fusion) could not be compiled for the Hailo-8 NPU due to unsupported operators.
The on-device comparison is therefore restricted to a subset of the candidate architectures,
and the relative embedded performance of the excluded models cannot be assessed.
The micro-fusion attention mechanisms were only evaluated within the mid-fusion architecture.
Their effect on other fusion architectures remains unknown.

The tracker comparison is carried out only on the 3D-Net dataset recorded at 6 fps under
favourable daytime conditions. The evaluation does not include sequences with adverse
weather or high vehicle density. This limits the generalisability of the ByteTrack
selection to more demanding operational scenarios. Additionally, the operational system
processes 20 fps video streams. The impact of this higher frame rate on tracker performance
is not evaluated.

Although the 3D-Net project targets a cooperative swarm of multiple UAVs, the project team
deliberately assigned vehicle tracking and congestion classification to a single drone
instance with one sensor system.
All experiments in this thesis are therefore conducted with a single UAV. Wider surveillance
coverage is achievable by moving UAVs along road segments and intersections, but the field
of view at any given time is limited to the sensor system of one drone. Multi-agent
perception scenarios, such as merging detections from overlapping fields of view or managing
vehicle hand-offs between agents, are not evaluated.

\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{chap:conclusion}

This thesis investigated the perception component of a UAV-based traffic monitoring system
operating under strict payload and power constraints. Two research questions guided the work.
The first asked whether reliable real-time vehicle detection is achievable on a UAV edge
device equipped with RGB and thermal sensors. The second asked what impact different sensor
fusion strategies have on detection performance in this context.

The evaluation confirms that real-time detection is achievable within the operational
constraints. All fusion architectures successfully compiled for the Hailo-8 NPU exceed the
20\,fps camera frame rate under INT8 quantization. The combined Raspberry Pi~5 and Hailo-8
system draws approximately 5\,W during inference. This represents 0.3\,\% of the total UAV
power draw and reduces practical flight time by under three seconds. The transition from
full-precision to INT8 quantization introduces small accuracy loss across all compiled
models. Together, these results confirm that the Hailo-8 platform is a viable inference
engine for multi-modal vehicle detection on a payload- and energy-constrained aerial
platform.

The comparison of seven fusion architectures shows that full-precision detection performance
is broadly similar across strategies despite large differences in model complexity. The
thermal modality contributes measurably in challenging conditions such as rain, fog, and
low light, but only in well-aligned scenarios. This confirms that multi-modal
sensing can add robustness beyond what an RGB-only
model can achieve. Late fusion is selected for deployment on the Hailo-8. It achieves the 
best overall
performance trade-off across all object sizes and lighting conditions and shows the best
quantized performance on the combined dataset while still achieving real-time performance
on the Hailo-8.

ByteTrack is identified as the appropriate tracker for this application. It achieves the
highest tracking accuracy across all evaluated methods, with a HOTA of 0.946 and an IDF1
of 0.954, while also achieving the lowest per-frame latency at 0.36\,ms on the
Raspberry~Pi~5 CPU. Re-identification features and camera motion compensation provide no
benefit under the predictable aerial viewpoint of this application, where objects follow
consistent trajectories with few sustained occlusions. Motion-based association in
combination with a high-quality detector is therefore sufficient.

The thesis makes three contributions to the 3D-Net project. First, the 3D-Net dataset
provides a low-altitude oblique paired RGB-LWIR benchmark for vehicle detection.
Second, to the best of our knowledge, this work presents the first
systematic comparison of multiple RGB-LWIR fusion architectures evaluated under INT8
quantization on a dedicated edge accelerator for aerial vehicle traffic monitoring. Third,
the modular YAML-configurable tracking service provides a message queue vehicle stream for the
downstream congestion classification and multi-agent coordination components of the broader
project.

Several directions remain open for future work. The 3D-Net dataset currently covers
only daytime, clear-weather sequences at moderate vehicle densities. An extension to adverse
weather, nighttime conditions, and higher traffic densities would broaden the evaluation
scope and make downstream congestion analysis feasible. Tracker performance has been assessed
at the 3D-Net datasets 6\,fps. A dedicated evaluation at the operational frame rate of 20\,fps under demanding
traffic conditions would confirm that latency and tracking quality hold under deployment
conditions. Three fusion architectures could not be compiled for the Hailo-8 due to
unsupported operators. An investigation of operator substitutions or modified architecture
variants for these models could resolve these incompatibilities and establish their effect
on quantized performance. The fusion strategy evaluation was conducted with YOLOv11n as the
base detector. The observed performance may be specific to this detector
family. An evaluation with alternative base detectors would establish its consistency across
architectures. The micro-fusion attention mechanisms were only explored within mid-level
fusion. It should be investigated whether other macro fusion architectures could benefit similarly.
Finally, this work does not address multi-agent tracking, which could
increase the simultaneous coverage of the monitored area. A dedicated study of
detection fusion across overlapping sensor fields of view and of vehicle identity continuity
across hand-offs between agents would close this gap.

\section{Bounding Box Rules}
\vspace{-0.5em}

Bounding boxes must be axis-aligned with the image, rotated boxes are not
supported. Only the visible portion of an object should be annotated. The
estimated extent of occluded areas must not be included. If a vehicle is
cut off at the image boundary, the bounding box stops at that boundary.

Side mirrors, open doors, and tailgates are part of the vehicle and are
included within the bounding box. Antennas and shadows are excluded.
If a vehicle is towing a trailer or similar attachment, a single bounding
box encompasses both the towing vehicle and the trailer, even if other
vehicles are loaded on the trailer.

\vspace{-2em}
\section{Minimum Requirements for Annotation}
\vspace{-0.5em}

A vehicle is annotated only when all of the following conditions are met.

\begin{itemize}
    \item The object occupies at least 16$\times$12 pixels in the image.
    \item At least 30\,\% of the vehicle is visible within the frame.
    \item The object can be identified with confidence from the current frame alone,
          knowledge of preceding or following frames must not be used.
    \item The object is clear enough to determine its class with certainty.
          If it is too blurry to classify, do not annotate it.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{-2em}
\section{Low-Light Conditions}
\vspace{-0.5em}

Objects that are difficult to distinguish due to low light should still be labeled.
The camera system includes a thermal camera that is unaffected by visible light and
provides clear object boundaries even at night, where vehicles and people are
thermally distinct from their surroundings.
When the visible channel is ambiguous, open the corresponding thermal image via
the S3 URL attached to the FiftyOne sample to confirm object presence and class.
